In the previous tutorial we began learning&nbsp;
about the most important class of minerals,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the silicates, and we examined&nbsp;
three of the silicate subclasses,&nbsp;&nbsp;
those being orthosilicates, disilicates, and&nbsp;
cyclosilicates. There are three more subclasses&nbsp;&nbsp;
to go over, so let’s talk about these now.
The next group of silicates, called chain&nbsp;&nbsp;
silicates, or inosilicates, are divided into two&nbsp;
subgroups: pyroxenes, which have single chains&nbsp;&nbsp;
of silica tetrahedra, and amphiboles, which&nbsp;
have double chains. Pyroxenes are composed&nbsp;&nbsp;
of silica tetrahedra linked together in a zig-zag&nbsp;
pattern, where one oxygen atom in each tetrahedron&nbsp;&nbsp;
is shared with the adjacent tetrahedron. They are&nbsp;
similar to ring silicates, in that each silica&nbsp;&nbsp;
tetrahedron shares two of its oxygens with other&nbsp;
silica tetrahedra, having a silicon to oxygen&nbsp;&nbsp;
ratio of 1 to 3. Pyroxenes have the general&nbsp;
formula XY(Si,Al)2O6 where the Y-cation is&nbsp;&nbsp;
a medium-sized cation in 6-fold coordination, and&nbsp;
the X cation is either a 6-fold or 8-fold cation,&nbsp;&nbsp;
depending on whether the crystal structure&nbsp;
is orthorhombic or monoclinic. Orthorhombic&nbsp;&nbsp;
pyroxenes are called orthopyroxenes, and they&nbsp;
have 6-fold cations on both their X and Y sites,&nbsp;&nbsp;
making them more symmetrical than monoclinic&nbsp;
pyroxenes, or clinopyroxenes, which have 8-fold&nbsp;&nbsp;
cations, typically calcium, on their X-site.
The orthopyroxene group only has two endmembers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the iron-rich ferrosilite, and magnesium-rich&nbsp;
enstatite, with complete solid solution between&nbsp;&nbsp;
them. The clinopyroxenes are more diverse&nbsp;
and widespread, forming a sizeable fraction&nbsp;&nbsp;
of most mafic rocks. Like orthopyroxene, there is&nbsp;
extensive solid solution among the clinopyroxenes.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Some endmember clinopyroxenes are the&nbsp;
calcic, magnesium-rich diopside and iron-rich&nbsp;&nbsp;
hedenbergite, the sodic, aluminum-rich jadeite&nbsp;
and iron-rich aegirine, and the lithium-bearing&nbsp;&nbsp;
spodumene. Gemstone-quality jadeite is used as&nbsp;
jade and spodumene is an important ore of lithium.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The most abundant pyroxene is augite, which is a&nbsp;
calcic pyroxene with an intermediate composition.&nbsp;
Double chain silicates, better known as&nbsp;
amphiboles, combine two single chains&nbsp;&nbsp;
of tetrahedra by having every other silica&nbsp;
tetrahedron share three of its oxygen atoms&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the adjacent tetrahedron, forming a ring-like&nbsp;
structure. They have a silicon to oxygen ratio of&nbsp;&nbsp;
1 to 2.5 and always have extra anions, typically&nbsp;
volatiles, like hydroxyl, chlorine, and fluorine.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Amphiboles can crystallize from a magma, but they&nbsp;
usually form during the hydrothermal alteration of&nbsp;&nbsp;
pyroxenes, via the reaction pyroxene + quartz +&nbsp;
water yields amphibole. There is an entire class&nbsp;&nbsp;
of metamorphic rocks called amphibolite because&nbsp;
their mineralogy is dominated by amphiboles.&nbsp;
Amphiboles have the general formula,&nbsp;
AB2C5(Si,Al)8O22W2, and are sometimes&nbsp;&nbsp;
called the “garbage can mineral”, because of their&nbsp;
ability to take in so many different elements.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The A, B, and C sites are distinguished based&nbsp;
on the size of cation they can accommodate,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the W-site contains hydroxyl, chlorine,&nbsp;
and fluorine. The A-site can either be vacant,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or contain the large cations potassium, sodium,&nbsp;
and/or calcium, the B-site contains medium-large&nbsp;&nbsp;
cations, such as calcium, sodium, and ferrous&nbsp;
iron, and the C-site is composed of strips&nbsp;&nbsp;
of octahedra that are sandwiched between the&nbsp;
chains of tetrahedra, gluing them together. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
octahedra are occupied by the medium-sized cations&nbsp;
magnesium, iron, and aluminum. Like the pyroxenes,&nbsp;&nbsp;
amphiboles are broken down into orthoamphiboles&nbsp;
and clinoamphiboles, and of course, there is&nbsp;&nbsp;
extensive solid solution among their respective&nbsp;
endmembers. Compared to clinoamphiboles,&nbsp;&nbsp;
orthoamphiboles are chemically simple, having a&nbsp;
vacant A-site, and only magnesium and iron in the&nbsp;&nbsp;
B-and C-sites. Clinoamphiboles are classified&nbsp;
based on the dominant cation in the B-site,&nbsp;&nbsp;
being either calcium amphiboles, sodium-calcium&nbsp;
amphiboles, or sodium amphiboles.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Blueschist, which is a class of metamorphic rock&nbsp;
common in subduction zones, gets its name from the&nbsp;&nbsp;
blue color of the sodium amphibole glaucophane&nbsp;
(Na2[Mg3Al2]Si8O22(OH)2). The medium-grade&nbsp;&nbsp;
metamorphic rock, amphibolite, typically&nbsp;
contains calcium amphiboles, such as hornblende.&nbsp;
Sheet silicates, or phyllosilicates, take&nbsp;
double chain silicates one step further. Each&nbsp;&nbsp;
silica tetrahedron shares 3 oxygen atoms with&nbsp;
the adjacent tetrahedron, forming an infinite&nbsp;&nbsp;
sheet of shared tetrahedra. Sheet silicates&nbsp;
have a silicon to oxygen ratio of 2 to 5.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The sheet of silica tetrahedra, referred to simply&nbsp;
as the tetrahedral, or ‘T” sheet is stacked on top&nbsp;&nbsp;
of an octahedral, or “O” sheet, which is a plane&nbsp;
of divalent and trivalent cations like iron,&nbsp;&nbsp;
magnesium, and aluminum. The T and O&nbsp;
sheets stack together to form layers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are bonded through strong, molecular&nbsp;
bonds. They can stack in one of two ways;&nbsp;&nbsp;
a T-O, or “open-faced sandwich” configuration,&nbsp;
with one T-sheet bonded to one O-sheet, or a&nbsp;&nbsp;
T-O-T configuration, where a single octahedral&nbsp;
sheet is sandwiched by two tetrahedral sheets. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
sheet silicates are a diverse group of minerals&nbsp;
that include the clay minerals, the micas, the&nbsp;&nbsp;
serpentine group, the chlorite group, and talc.
The differences between the groups of sheet&nbsp;&nbsp;
silicates are the types of bonding&nbsp;
that hold together the sheeted layers.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For example, in the mineral talc, Mg3Si2O5(OH)4,&nbsp;
they are held together by van der Waals forces,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are relatively weak. It is no coincidence&nbsp;
that talc, with a Mohs hardness of 1 is the&nbsp;&nbsp;
softest mineral; so soft in fact, that it is&nbsp;
used to make baby powder. On the other hand,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the micas are held together by weak ionic bonds&nbsp;
that arise from electrostatic interactions&nbsp;&nbsp;
between large, interlayer cations, like potassium,&nbsp;
and the negatively charged T-O-T layers.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This structure is called T-O-T + C, with the&nbsp;
“C” standing for cation. The difference between&nbsp;&nbsp;
these two groups are the tetrahedral cations; in&nbsp;
talc, only Si4+ occupies the tetrahedral sites,&nbsp;&nbsp;
while in the micas, one Si4+ swaps for Al3+,&nbsp;
creating a net negative charge among the layers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and allowing for stronger electrostatic bonds&nbsp;
between the layers, with the large cations acting&nbsp;&nbsp;
as the glue. The chlorite minerals have a similar&nbsp;
structure, with negatively charged layers due to&nbsp;&nbsp;
aluminum substitution; however, instead of being&nbsp;
glued together by cations, they have an additional&nbsp;&nbsp;
positively charged octahedral sheet between&nbsp;
layers. This structure is called T-O-T + O.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The last group has a T-O structure, and like&nbsp;
talc, the layers are neutral, but are in this&nbsp;&nbsp;
case held together by stronger hydrogen bonds.&nbsp;
It turns out that most of the sheet silicates&nbsp;&nbsp;
have a similar hardness, between about 2 and&nbsp;
3, except for the T-O-T group, which are the&nbsp;&nbsp;
softest of all the minerals. The weak bonding&nbsp;
between layers gives all the sheet silicates&nbsp;&nbsp;
perfect basal cleavage, causing them to peel&nbsp;
apart like the plies in a roll of toilet paper.&nbsp;
We will now move on to the most abundant&nbsp;
subclass of minerals in the Earth’s crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the framework silicates, or tectosilicates. These&nbsp;
minerals have a backbone of silica tetrahedra&nbsp;&nbsp;
that share all four of their oxygens with&nbsp;
neighboring silica tetrahedra. The framework&nbsp;&nbsp;
silicates have a silicon to oxygen ratio of 1 to&nbsp;
2, with aluminum often substituting for silicon in&nbsp;&nbsp;
the tetrahedral sites, as in the feldspar mineral&nbsp;
anorthite, where half its tetrahedral sites are&nbsp;&nbsp;
occupied by aluminum. The complete polymerization&nbsp;
of tetrahedra creates a complex, rigid structure&nbsp;&nbsp;
with large spaces in between, allowing for&nbsp;
a wide range of interstitial large cations.&nbsp;
The basic chemical formula for&nbsp;
feldspars is (Ca,Na,K)(Si,Al)4O8,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and can be separated into three&nbsp;
endmembers: anorthite, CaAl2Si2O8,&nbsp;&nbsp;
albite, NaAlSi3O8, and potassium feldspar,&nbsp;
KAlSi3O8. There is complete solid solution&nbsp;&nbsp;
between albite and anorthite, with intermediate&nbsp;
compositions being called plagioclase. There is&nbsp;&nbsp;
also complete solid solution between albite and&nbsp;
potassium feldspar, being called alkali feldspar.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For two isostructural minerals to have solid&nbsp;
solution, their cations must meet one of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
two following criteria: One, they have a similar&nbsp;
ionic radius, or two, they have the same charge.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Since potassium and calcium do not meet these&nbsp;
criteria, solid solution does not occur between&nbsp;&nbsp;
anorthite and potassium feldspar. Plagioclase&nbsp;
is the most abundant mineral in the crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
being a main component of both the continental&nbsp;
and oceanic crust. Another important framework&nbsp;&nbsp;
silicate is quartz, which is composed&nbsp;
entirely of silica tetrahedra, having&nbsp;&nbsp;
the formula SiO2. The silicon-oxygen covalent&nbsp;
bond is one of the strongest single bonds,&nbsp;&nbsp;
making quartz and the other framework silicates&nbsp;
very hard. Quartz is actually a polymorph of SiO2,&nbsp;&nbsp;
meaning its structure changes with pressure and&nbsp;
temperature. Tridymite and cristobalite are the&nbsp;&nbsp;
high-temperature polymorphs, with coesite and&nbsp;
stishovite being the high-pressure polymorphs.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Stishovite can only be formed at pressures&nbsp;
greater than 10 gigapascals and is often&nbsp;&nbsp;
used to identify ancient asteroid impacts.
With that we conclude our introduction to&nbsp;&nbsp;
the silicates, and to the classes of minerals in&nbsp;
general. But how do these minerals form and come&nbsp;&nbsp;
together to produce rocks? Let’s move forward&nbsp;
and begin to answer precisely this question.
