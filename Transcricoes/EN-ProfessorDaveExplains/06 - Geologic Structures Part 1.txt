We just got finished discussing the four eons of&nbsp;
geologic time, those being the Hadean, Archean,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Proterozoic, and Phanerozoic. Now let’s get&nbsp;
more specific with the geologic structures&nbsp;&nbsp;
that span those eons. We’re going to explore how&nbsp;
rocks on Earth can change their shape, thereby&nbsp;&nbsp;
creating dramatic landforms over the millennia&nbsp;
that field geologists love to study today.&nbsp;
Understanding how rocks deform requires&nbsp;
a basic understanding of the mechanical&nbsp;&nbsp;
properties of geologic materials and how these&nbsp;
mechanical properties can change under different&nbsp;&nbsp;
pressure-temperature conditions. Since both&nbsp;
pressure and temperature always increase with&nbsp;&nbsp;
depth, rocks in the shallow crust are deformed&nbsp;
differently than deeper ones, thereby forming&nbsp;&nbsp;
different structures than rocks in the deep crust.&nbsp;
We live at the very top of the crust, and to us,&nbsp;&nbsp;
rocks are very rigid objects that are&nbsp;
often used as a building material. However,&nbsp;&nbsp;
if the surface of the Earth was at a temperature&nbsp;
of 1400°C and a pressure of 100,000 atmospheres,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we would find rocks behaving a bit like silly&nbsp;
putty, or at least like glacial ice. Glaciers&nbsp;&nbsp;
are composed of solid ice crystals; and&nbsp;
in spite of this, they are able to flow,&nbsp;&nbsp;
even if at a very slow rate. This is how rocks&nbsp;
behave in the mantle. The term viscosity relates&nbsp;&nbsp;
to a material’s resistance to flow, and it is&nbsp;
a very important concept in structural geology.&nbsp;
Now, to better understand the mechanical&nbsp;
behavior of rocks, we need to understand the&nbsp;&nbsp;
concepts of stress and strain. Stress describes&nbsp;
the quantity of the forces that cause deformation&nbsp;&nbsp;
and is expressed in terms of force per unit area,&nbsp;
or Pascals. Stress is equivalent to the pressure&nbsp;&nbsp;
of a solid. There are three normal components of&nbsp;
stress, with normal meaning acting perpendicular&nbsp;&nbsp;
to a surface, and each one describes the&nbsp;
amount of stress in a particular direction&nbsp;&nbsp;
in three-dimensional space. If a force pulls on&nbsp;
an object in an outward direction, causing the&nbsp;&nbsp;
object to stretch, that is called tensile stress.&nbsp;
Conversely, if a force pushes inward on an object,&nbsp;&nbsp;
causing it to squash, that is called compressive&nbsp;
stress. Both compressive and tensile stresses are&nbsp;&nbsp;
normal stresses. Forces that act parallel&nbsp;
to a surface are called shear stress.&nbsp;&nbsp;
If an object experiences sufficient stress to&nbsp;
cause deformation, whether it is stretched,&nbsp;&nbsp;
squashed, or sheared, it undergoes&nbsp;
strain. Strain is a unitless measure&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the change in shape or volume of an object.
The relationship between stress and strain, or&nbsp;&nbsp;
how a material responds to stress, is represented&nbsp;
by a value called Young’s modulus. Young’s modulus&nbsp;&nbsp;
is dependent on the physical properties of a&nbsp;
material and is determined in the laboratory by&nbsp;&nbsp;
subjecting it to a series of different pressures&nbsp;
and measuring the accompanying strain. These&nbsp;&nbsp;
values are recorded and used to create a graph&nbsp;
for the material’s stress-strain relationship.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The slope of the best-fitting line through&nbsp;
the data is the material’s Young’s modulus.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Since minerals are the building blocks of rocks&nbsp;
and each mineral has a specific value for Young’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
modulus, the mineralogy of a given rock will exert&nbsp;
a strong control on how that rock will deform.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The relative tendency of a rock&nbsp;
to strain in response to stress&nbsp;&nbsp;
is called its competence, which can also&nbsp;
be defined as the relative viscosity of a&nbsp;&nbsp;
rock. Incompetent rocks are composed&nbsp;
of soft, weak minerals like clay,&nbsp;&nbsp;
while competent rocks are composed of strong,&nbsp;
rigid minerals like garnet or feldspar.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Other than competence, the manner in which a rock&nbsp;
responds to stress is controlled by the conditions&nbsp;&nbsp;
that it is under, mainly temperature and pressure,&nbsp;
but other factors like the deformation rate and&nbsp;&nbsp;
presence of fluids are also important. As the&nbsp;
pressure and temperature of a rock are increased,&nbsp;&nbsp;
its strength, or viscosity decreases,&nbsp;
making it more willing to deform.&nbsp;
We now understand that rocks respond to stress by&nbsp;
becoming strained. The precise response to stress&nbsp;&nbsp;
is described by four different mechanisms:&nbsp;
brittle deformation, elastic deformation, plastic&nbsp;&nbsp;
deformation, and viscous deformation. Viscous&nbsp;
deformation mainly describes the deformation&nbsp;&nbsp;
of fluids. Rocks that deform in a brittle manner&nbsp;
break instead of bend, and this behavior is most&nbsp;&nbsp;
common at the surface. In fact, most rocks in the&nbsp;
upper 10 kilometers or so of crust tend to deform&nbsp;&nbsp;
via mechanisms of brittle deformation. This is&nbsp;
called the brittle regime. Here, fracturing and&nbsp;&nbsp;
faulting are the main expressions of stress,&nbsp;
though elastic deformation is also very common.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Elastic deformation is the manifestation of the&nbsp;
bending of atomic bonds that occurs when an object&nbsp;&nbsp;
is stressed. When a force is exerted on an object,&nbsp;
its atomic bonds stretch parallel to the maximum&nbsp;&nbsp;
tensile force and contract parallel to the maximum&nbsp;
compressive force. If the force is increased,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the material experiences more strain, and once&nbsp;
the force is removed, the material reverts back&nbsp;&nbsp;
to its original shape when the atomic bonds relax.&nbsp;
However, it is possible to exert a force so great,&nbsp;&nbsp;
that the rock fails and forms a fracture. When a&nbsp;
rock fails under stress, all of the accumulated&nbsp;&nbsp;
stress that was responsible for pulling the&nbsp;
atoms apart is released, causing an earthquake.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This is like how if you overstretch a&nbsp;
rubber band, which deforms elastically,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the released energy snaps the broken ends of the&nbsp;
rubber band back toward your hand when it breaks.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Therefore, we would expect the largest earthquakes&nbsp;
to occur in the hardest and most competent rocks.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Subduction zones are known for their massive&nbsp;
earthquakes. This is because they are able to&nbsp;&nbsp;
transport a very competent material, oceanic&nbsp;
crust, to great depths and therefore great&nbsp;&nbsp;
pressures. Normally, materials at these depths&nbsp;
are too weak to cause earthquakes due to their&nbsp;&nbsp;
high temperature, causing them to bend instead&nbsp;
of break. This brings us to plastic deformation,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which describes how weak materials deform, like&nbsp;
silly putty. In contrast with elastic deformation,&nbsp;&nbsp;
plastic deformation occurs when atomic&nbsp;
bonds are broken under stress. Under&nbsp;&nbsp;
perfect plastic deformation, materials do&nbsp;
not accumulate energy as they’re stressed.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Again, this is because the energy is released when&nbsp;
the atomic bonds are broken. Materials that deform&nbsp;&nbsp;
plastically do not revert to their original shape&nbsp;
once the stress is removed. Plastic deformation&nbsp;&nbsp;
occurs mainly under the enormous pressures and&nbsp;
temperatures of the lower crust and mantle, though&nbsp;&nbsp;
it can occur closer to the surface if either&nbsp;
the temperature is high enough or the material&nbsp;&nbsp;
is weak enough, for instance, beneath volcanoes.
That covers an introduction to stress and strain,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as well as the types of rock deformation. But&nbsp;
we have a bit more to talk about in this realm,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so let’s move forward and look&nbsp;
at some specific structures.
