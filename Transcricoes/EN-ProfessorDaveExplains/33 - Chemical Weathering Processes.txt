In the previous tutorial, we learned about&nbsp;
physical weathering, which breaks down rock&nbsp;&nbsp;
via mechanical processes. So now let’s move on&nbsp;
to chemical weathering. Chemical weathering is&nbsp;&nbsp;
the collection of processes that cause changes&nbsp;
to rocks and minerals on the molecular level. As&nbsp;&nbsp;
a general rule of thumb, minerals with higher&nbsp;
crystallization temperatures tend to weather&nbsp;&nbsp;
faster at the surface. Chemical weathering&nbsp;
almost exclusively involves reactions between&nbsp;&nbsp;
minerals and natural, aqueous solutions. There&nbsp;
are two types of chemical weathering reactions,&nbsp;&nbsp;
congruent and incongruent. Congruent&nbsp;
reactions are the simple dissolution&nbsp;&nbsp;
of minerals into their constituent ions.&nbsp;
Take for example the dissolution of halite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or sodium chloride, which breaks down&nbsp;
into Na+ and Cl- in water. By contrast,&nbsp;&nbsp;
incongruent reactions produce entirely new&nbsp;
minerals or amorphous solids. For example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the sodium feldspar albite reacts with water&nbsp;
and carbon dioxide to form kaolinite clay&nbsp;&nbsp;
and dissolved sodium ions, bicarbonate ions, and&nbsp;
silicic acid, as is shown by this equation here.&nbsp;
The most important chemical weathering reactions&nbsp;
are acid-base reactions, which involve the&nbsp;&nbsp;
exchange of protons, and redox reactions, which&nbsp;
involve the exchange of electrons. These should&nbsp;&nbsp;
both be very familiar from our study of general&nbsp;
chemistry. Before we get into acid-base reactions,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we must consider that every drop of rainwater&nbsp;
contains some dissolved atmospheric gases,&nbsp;&nbsp;
those being nitrogen, oxygen, argon, and most&nbsp;
importantly carbon dioxide. In the atmosphere,&nbsp;&nbsp;
water reacts with dissolved carbon&nbsp;
dioxide to form carbonic acid, or H2CO3,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and any water in equilibrium with the atmosphere&nbsp;
will have a naturally acidic pH of around 5.6.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Areas with bad air pollution will have rain&nbsp;
that is even more acidic, due the formation of&nbsp;&nbsp;
sulfuric acid and nitric acid from the reaction&nbsp;
of rainwater with hydrocarbon combustion gases.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Furthermore, biological activity in the shallow&nbsp;
subsurface releases additional CO2 that gets&nbsp;&nbsp;
dissolved in the groundwater, further decreasing&nbsp;
its pH to around 4 or 5. As we know from learning&nbsp;&nbsp;
about acids and bases, acidic solutions have a&nbsp;
surplus of hydronium ions. It is these ions that&nbsp;&nbsp;
react with minerals to break them down. Recall&nbsp;
the chemical weathering of albite, this is an&nbsp;&nbsp;
acid-base reaction where it is attacked and broken&nbsp;
down by hydrogen ions, causing the release of&nbsp;&nbsp;
sodium ions and silicic acid into solution. Since&nbsp;
many weathering reactions involve carbon dioxide,&nbsp;&nbsp;
bicarbonate ions are a common product. As a&nbsp;
result, bicarbonate is by far the most abundant&nbsp;&nbsp;
anion in natural waters. The production of&nbsp;
bicarbonate during weathering also acts to&nbsp;&nbsp;
neutralize rainwater’s pH as it infiltrates deeper&nbsp;
into the ground, causing groundwater to become&nbsp;&nbsp;
either neutral or slightly basic. Therefore, most&nbsp;
chemical weathering occurs near the surface when&nbsp;&nbsp;
groundwater is most acidic and therefore most&nbsp;
reactive. The chemical weathering of silicate&nbsp;&nbsp;
rocks is the most important long-term mechanism&nbsp;
for removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere&nbsp;&nbsp;
via its conversion to bicarbonate and eventual&nbsp;
transport to the ocean basins where it reacts with&nbsp;&nbsp;
calcium to form calcite, then sinking down to the&nbsp;
bottom of the ocean where it is stored for eons.&nbsp;
Chemical weathering involving redox reactions&nbsp;
is extremely important when transition metals,&nbsp;&nbsp;
especially iron, are involved. At the most basic&nbsp;
level, chemical weathering occurs because most&nbsp;&nbsp;
minerals form deep within the Earth, where the&nbsp;
pressure and temperature are orders of magnitude&nbsp;&nbsp;
greater than at the surface. Thus, when they&nbsp;
are exhumed, they are often unstable and break&nbsp;&nbsp;
down into minerals that are more stable under&nbsp;
these new conditions. The difference between&nbsp;&nbsp;
the pressure and temperature at the surface&nbsp;
and deep within the lithosphere is great,&nbsp;&nbsp;
between 2 and 4 orders of magnitude, but this&nbsp;
is nothing compared to the difference in oxygen&nbsp;&nbsp;
concentration, which can be over 15 orders&nbsp;
of magnitude. Because of this, compounds that&nbsp;&nbsp;
contain metals with multiple oxidation states&nbsp;
become oxidized, or in other words, get some of&nbsp;&nbsp;
their valence electrons stolen by oxygen.
Weathering-related oxidation is extremely&nbsp;&nbsp;
common in iron-containing minerals like olivine,&nbsp;
pyroxene, and biotite. For example, fayalite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the iron endmember of olivine, is first attacked&nbsp;
by acidic waters, breaking it down to silicic acid&nbsp;&nbsp;
and dissolved Fe2+ ions. Next, aqueous Fe2+&nbsp;
has one of its electrons stolen by oxygen and&nbsp;&nbsp;
becomes Fe3+, which then reacts with water to from&nbsp;
precipitates such as amorphous ferric hydroxide,&nbsp;&nbsp;
goethite, or FeO(OH), and hematite, or Fe2O3.&nbsp;
Compounds of ferric iron are notoriously insoluble&nbsp;&nbsp;
and are often responsible for the red color in&nbsp;
rocks. Another mineral that weathers via oxidation&nbsp;&nbsp;
is pyrite, which contains one Fe2+ ion and two&nbsp;
S- ions. In the presence of oxygen-rich waters,&nbsp;&nbsp;
both iron and sulfur are oxidized and react with&nbsp;
water to form ferric hydroxide and sulfuric acid.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The creation of sulfuric acid through this&nbsp;
and similar reactions is responsible for&nbsp;&nbsp;
destructive acid mine drainage that can damage&nbsp;
ecosystems and contaminate drinking water.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Acid mine drainage is doubly harmful, because&nbsp;
not only is the acidic water harmful, but it&nbsp;&nbsp;
also is capable of dissolving large amounts of&nbsp;
metals, thereby further contaminating rivers and&nbsp;&nbsp;
streams. The characteristic yellow color&nbsp;
of acid mine drainage, called yellow boy,&nbsp;&nbsp;
is caused by Fe3+ and other metals dropping&nbsp;
out of solution and forming precipitates&nbsp;&nbsp;
as its pH rises outside of the mine and it is&nbsp;
progressively diluted by normal surface water.&nbsp;

physical and chemical weathering processes.&nbsp;&nbsp;
