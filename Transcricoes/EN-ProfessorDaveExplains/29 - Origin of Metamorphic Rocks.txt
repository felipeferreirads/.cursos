In this series, we have learned about how&nbsp;
minerals crystallize from liquid hot magma&nbsp;&nbsp;
to form igneous rocks, which over time,&nbsp;
are uplifted, broken down into sediments,&nbsp;&nbsp;
transported, and eventually deposited in a basin,&nbsp;
where they are buried, compacted, and cemented to&nbsp;&nbsp;
become sedimentary rock. In this tutorial, we&nbsp;
will discuss what happens when a sedimentary&nbsp;&nbsp;
or igneous rock gets squashed in between two&nbsp;
colliding plates, and the tremendous pressures&nbsp;&nbsp;
and temperatures that result cause the original&nbsp;
rock to recrystallize. We are of course talking&nbsp;&nbsp;
about metamorphism, and this is the origin of our&nbsp;
third and final type of rock, metamorphic rock.&nbsp;
Metamorphism is defined as any change in&nbsp;
the mineralogy or physical structure of&nbsp;&nbsp;
a rock by natural processes, such as increased&nbsp;
pressure and temperature, or the introduction&nbsp;&nbsp;
of chemically active fluids, without melting it&nbsp;
into magma. To truly understand metamorphism,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we must understand that every mineral has a range&nbsp;
of physical conditions, such as temperature and&nbsp;&nbsp;
pressure, as well as chemical conditions, over&nbsp;
which it is stable. When the conditions move&nbsp;&nbsp;
out of a mineral’s stability range, it will&nbsp;
break down into other, more stable minerals.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This is why igneous rocks undergo chemical&nbsp;
weathering at the surface. Minerals that&nbsp;&nbsp;
form in an igneous environment are stable at very&nbsp;
high temperatures where both oxygen and water are&nbsp;&nbsp;
scarce, but they are not stable in the water and&nbsp;
oxygen rich conditions at the surface. Therefore,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they react to form more stable minerals&nbsp;
like clay. Diagenesis can also be thought&nbsp;&nbsp;
of as super-low-grade metamorphism, where the&nbsp;
slightly increased pressures and temperatures&nbsp;&nbsp;
deep within a sedimentary basin can cause&nbsp;
recrystallization of minerals like calcite.&nbsp;&nbsp;
However, when geologists talk about metamorphism,&nbsp;
they are referring to rocks that recrystallize&nbsp;&nbsp;
at temperatures greater than 200 degrees&nbsp;
Celsius, far above the realm of diagenesis.&nbsp;
Metamorphism is classified based on both its&nbsp;
intensity, and its pressure with respect to&nbsp;&nbsp;
temperature, which corresponds to the tectonic&nbsp;
environment where metamorphism took place.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Metamorphic intensity can be classified as either&nbsp;
low grade, medium grade, or high grade, with low&nbsp;&nbsp;
grade describing the pressure and temperature&nbsp;
conditions above the realm of diagenesis,&nbsp;&nbsp;
high-grade encompassing the conditions up until&nbsp;
melting begins, and medium grade being in between.&nbsp;&nbsp;
When examining a metamorphic rock, its&nbsp;
metamorphic grade can be determined by the&nbsp;&nbsp;
amounts of recrystallization and foliation. To put&nbsp;
this into context, let’s take a mudstone, which&nbsp;&nbsp;
is a sedimentary rock primarily composed of clay&nbsp;
minerals, and progressively metamorphose the rock.&nbsp;&nbsp;
As we increase the temperature to around 300&nbsp;
degrees Celsius and add a few kilobars of&nbsp;&nbsp;
pressure, the platy and elongate minerals will&nbsp;
begin to align perpendicular to the direction&nbsp;&nbsp;
of greatest stress. This transformation is most&nbsp;
radical in rocks that are rich in sheet silicates,&nbsp;&nbsp;
like clay, muscovite, and biotite. In rocks,&nbsp;
this alignment of platy minerals is said to&nbsp;&nbsp;
create foliation, or cleavage, and the beginning&nbsp;
of the development of cleavage is what defines a&nbsp;&nbsp;
low-grade metamorphic rock, which is called&nbsp;
slate. Slate makes great building stones&nbsp;&nbsp;
because of its good cleavage, which causes&nbsp;
it to break along nice, planar surfaces.&nbsp;
As we continue to ramp up the pressure and&nbsp;
temperature to around 400 degrees Celsius&nbsp;&nbsp;
and 5 kilobars, the clay minerals will begin&nbsp;
to recrystallize into other sheet silicates,&nbsp;&nbsp;
most commonly muscovite and chlorite, which will&nbsp;
grow with their sheets, or basal cleavage, lining&nbsp;&nbsp;
up perpendicular to the main stress, further&nbsp;
enhancing the rock’s cleavage. These medium-grade&nbsp;&nbsp;
metamorphic rocks are distinguished from slate by&nbsp;
the onset of recrystallization, which gives it a&nbsp;&nbsp;
lustrous sheen. At the lower end of medium-grade&nbsp;
metamorphism, the recrystallized micas are very&nbsp;&nbsp;
fine-grained, having the appearance of glitter.&nbsp;
This type of rock is called phyllite. Closer to&nbsp;&nbsp;
the upper limit, recrystallization increases to&nbsp;
the point where the individual mica grains become&nbsp;&nbsp;
easily visible to the naked eye. This rock is&nbsp;
called schist. In addition to mica, schists may&nbsp;&nbsp;
contain other visible minerals, such as garnet&nbsp;
dodecahedra or prismatic grains of amphibole.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Once the temperature and pressure surpass around&nbsp;
600 degrees Celsius and 8 kilobars, the previously&nbsp;&nbsp;
stable chlorite and muscovite become unstable and&nbsp;
react with quartz to form more stable minerals&nbsp;&nbsp;
such as potassium feldspar, garnet, sillimanite,&nbsp;
and biotite. The characteristic feature of&nbsp;&nbsp;
high-grade metamorphic rocks is the segregation&nbsp;
of the mafic and felsic minerals during&nbsp;&nbsp;
recrystallization, which manifests as alternating&nbsp;
bands of light and dark minerals. Rocks that&nbsp;&nbsp;
display this type of foliation are called gneiss.
Now that we understand how metamorphic rocks form,&nbsp;&nbsp;
let’s move forward and get a closer look&nbsp;
at the different types of metamorphism,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so we can better understand the&nbsp;
different types of metamorphic rocks.
