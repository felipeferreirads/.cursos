This is Earth, a rocky planet, covered with&nbsp;
liquid water, orbiting within the habitable&nbsp;&nbsp;
zone of a very typical star. From afar it may&nbsp;
not look like much, but if you get close enough,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it becomes quite clear that there is a lot to&nbsp;
study on this little blue world. Some choose to&nbsp;&nbsp;
study botany, to learn about the wide variety&nbsp;
of plant life. Some study zoology and learn&nbsp;&nbsp;
about all the animals walking on the land,&nbsp;
flying in the air, and swimming in the ocean.&nbsp;&nbsp;
But some choose to study the Earth itself.
What is the precise composition of the Earth?&nbsp;&nbsp;
What is its history? The people who answer&nbsp;
these questions are geologists. Now when&nbsp;&nbsp;
most people think of a geologist, they tend&nbsp;
to imagine someone examining a bunch of rocks,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and of course, geologists do indeed study rocks;&nbsp;
but geology is also much more than this. To&nbsp;&nbsp;
understand why earthquakes occur or how volcanoes&nbsp;
form, one must do more than simply look at rocks.&nbsp;&nbsp;
At its core, geology is a multidisciplinary&nbsp;
science that combines chemistry, physics,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and mathematics to investigate an enormous variety&nbsp;
of processes that occur on and in the Earth.&nbsp;&nbsp;
As we will come to understand in this series,&nbsp;
geology encompasses every aspect of Earth’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
structure, from the molten iron core to the&nbsp;
beautiful minerals we find on its surface.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Much of what geologists study transpired over&nbsp;
incredibly long timescales, originating anywhere&nbsp;&nbsp;
from thousands to billions of years in the past.
Geologic processes have shaped the entirety of&nbsp;&nbsp;
Earth’s surface. For example, the Appalachian&nbsp;
Mountains of Eastern North America contain&nbsp;&nbsp;
limestones, which are composed of the shells of&nbsp;
marine animals that lived there, in a shallow&nbsp;&nbsp;
ocean more than 400 million years ago. Then, 300&nbsp;
million years ago, those rock beds were thrust&nbsp;&nbsp;
upwards as Earth’s tectonic plates began to&nbsp;
converge along the eastern US coast. Later, during&nbsp;&nbsp;
glacial periods between 2 million and 12,000 years&nbsp;
ago, mile-thick ice sheets scraped off the tops&nbsp;&nbsp;
of these mountains, like huge bulldozers,&nbsp;
as they moved southward, then deposited&nbsp;&nbsp;
hundreds of feet of sand and gravel in stream&nbsp;
valleys as they retreated northward and melted.&nbsp;
How is it that we can know these things, exactly?&nbsp;
And furthermore, why even bother studying geology&nbsp;&nbsp;
if all these phenomena occur on a much longer&nbsp;
time scale than humans can observe during their&nbsp;&nbsp;
lifetimes? The simple answer is that the processes&nbsp;
that shaped our planet billions of years ago,&nbsp;&nbsp;
still occur today and can be easily observed and&nbsp;
measured. Next time you visit the beach, pay close&nbsp;&nbsp;
attention to the patterns of the sand, and observe&nbsp;
them as they form from wind and water currents.&nbsp;&nbsp;
These patterns are exactly what we&nbsp;
find in many ancient sandstones,&nbsp;&nbsp;
therefore it is reasonable to assume&nbsp;
they were created by the same processes,&nbsp;&nbsp;
just at different points in time. This is the&nbsp;
essence of geology. Observing beach sand form&nbsp;&nbsp;
ripples and dunes may not be the most exciting&nbsp;
activity to most, so you might be wondering,&nbsp;&nbsp;
who would be interested in geology? The first&nbsp;
recorded dabbling in geology was by Aristotle and&nbsp;&nbsp;
the ancient Greeks. These ancient philosophers&nbsp;
wanted to understand natural phenomena,&nbsp;&nbsp;
especially those impactful to human life,&nbsp;
like earthquakes, volcanoes, and tidal waves.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Aristotle thought earthquakes were caused by&nbsp;
opposing winds within the cavernous Earth, while&nbsp;&nbsp;
Democritus thought the cause was water forcing&nbsp;
its way into the Earth as it rains. Obviously,&nbsp;&nbsp;
these are both incorrect, and we now know&nbsp;
earthquakes to be caused by rocks cracking under&nbsp;&nbsp;
the enormous stress created by plate tectonics,&nbsp;
but the desire to understand is ancient.&nbsp;
Geology has come a long way since the time&nbsp;
of Aristotle and has many applications toward&nbsp;&nbsp;
improving and protecting human life. For example,&nbsp;
volcanologists, or geologists who study volcanoes,&nbsp;&nbsp;
closely monitor active ones, and create early&nbsp;
warning systems to evacuate towns before any&nbsp;&nbsp;
volcanic eruptions. Furthermore, Earth contains&nbsp;
minerals, metals, and sources of energy that we&nbsp;&nbsp;
have used to build and power the technology&nbsp;
and infrastructure responsible for raising&nbsp;&nbsp;
our collective standard of living over the past&nbsp;
few hundred years. In fact, many geologists work&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the resource industry, finding new places&nbsp;
or developing new ways to extract minerals and&nbsp;&nbsp;
energy. Others assess hazards to human life,&nbsp;
like the volcanic eruptions we mentioned,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but also slope failures, massive floods, and&nbsp;
structural damage from earthquakes. Biologists&nbsp;&nbsp;
interested in the evolution of life on Earth work&nbsp;
closely with geologists in looking at the rock&nbsp;&nbsp;
record to find the fossils and footprints&nbsp;
of organisms that are now long extinct.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Climate scientists interested in understanding&nbsp;
our impact on the environment measure, among&nbsp;&nbsp;
other things, greenhouse gases trapped inside&nbsp;
tiny bubbles from 800,000-year-old ice cores.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The impact doesn’t even stop at our own planet.&nbsp;
The understanding of Earth provided by geologists&nbsp;&nbsp;
acts as the framework that planetary scientists&nbsp;
use to study outflow channels on Mars, crater&nbsp;&nbsp;
topography on the Moon, and unique features of&nbsp;
exoplanets that are continually being discovered.&nbsp;
So, as you can see, geology is not just about&nbsp;
looking at rocks. It is as rich and multi-faceted&nbsp;&nbsp;
as any other scientific discipline. As we&nbsp;
mentioned, geology utilizes concepts in chemistry,&nbsp;&nbsp;
physics, and mathematics, and an understanding of&nbsp;
basic concepts from these fields will be assumed&nbsp;&nbsp;
as we move through this geology series. When we&nbsp;

we won’t be reviewing chemistry concepts&nbsp;

and when we talk about large-scale geological&nbsp;

regarding forces and energy, nor the basic&nbsp;
algebra that is sometimes required to discuss&nbsp;&nbsp;
these phenomena quantitatively. So, if you need&nbsp;
