At this point in the series, we understand the&nbsp;
structure of the Earth, which includes the crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and we know that there is crust below our feet,&nbsp;
but also oceanic crust at the bottom of the ocean.&nbsp;&nbsp;
We mentioned a few of the features of oceanic&nbsp;
crust when we introduced plate tectonics,&nbsp;&nbsp;
those being mid-ocean ridges and trenches. But&nbsp;
to fully understand the structure of oceanic&nbsp;&nbsp;
crust, it will be useful to learn more&nbsp;
about the layers of the ocean itself.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Water covers over 70% of the Earth’s&nbsp;
surface, so if we want to know about&nbsp;&nbsp;
every area of Earth’s surface, this will&nbsp;
indeed be a critical section to include.&nbsp;
Let’s take a deep dive into the Pacific Ocean.&nbsp;
The western edge of the Pacific Ocean starts with&nbsp;&nbsp;
a ramp of continental crust that extends off the&nbsp;
coast of mainland Japan to the deep Japan trench.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Scientists call this ramp of continental&nbsp;
crust beneath the ocean the continental&nbsp;&nbsp;
shelf. Typically, continental shelves are broad,&nbsp;
sloping plains covered in shallow ocean water less&nbsp;&nbsp;
than 250 meters deep, but in the Pacific Ocean,&nbsp;
the gentle gradient of the continental shelf&nbsp;&nbsp;
drops off quickly into a steep incline called&nbsp;
the continental slope. Off the coast of Japan,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the continental slope extends all the way&nbsp;
down to the bottom of the Japan trench,&nbsp;&nbsp;
over 7 kilometers in depth.
As the depth changes along&nbsp;&nbsp;
the continental shelf and slope, the amount of&nbsp;
sunlight that penetrates through the ocean water&nbsp;&nbsp;
changes drastically. At the shallow depths along&nbsp;
the continental shelf, between 0 and 200 meters,&nbsp;&nbsp;
there is enough light present for photosynthesis&nbsp;
to take place. Physical oceanographers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
scientists who study the interactions of the&nbsp;
ocean with light, wind, and rock, have called&nbsp;&nbsp;
this top layer the photic or epipelagic zone.&nbsp;
Pelagic means open ocean and refers only to&nbsp;&nbsp;
areas that are far from the influence of the&nbsp;
shore and the ocean floor. 90% of ocean life,&nbsp;&nbsp;
from whales and dolphins to green algae and&nbsp;
phytoplankton, lives within the epipelagic zone.&nbsp;
Beneath the epipelagic zone is the mesopelagic&nbsp;
zone, which extends from 200 to 1000 meters,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where sunlight can no longer reach. Small&nbsp;
bristlemouth fish make their home here&nbsp;&nbsp;
and migrate up to the epipelagic zone under the&nbsp;
cover of night to feed. Although most people have&nbsp;&nbsp;
never even seen one, the tan bristlemouth fish is&nbsp;
the most abundant vertebrate on Earth, so they are&nbsp;&nbsp;
clearly thriving in their deep-sea environment.
Next is the bathypelagic zone, from 1000 to 4000&nbsp;&nbsp;
meters. Sunlight certainly can’t reach&nbsp;
the bathypelagic zone and the organisms&nbsp;&nbsp;
there live in complete darkness, unable to see&nbsp;
anything. Some species, like the anglerfish,&nbsp;&nbsp;
use the lack of sunlight to their advantage&nbsp;
and create specks of light from symbiotic,&nbsp;&nbsp;
bioluminescent bacteria living at the&nbsp;
tip of their dorsal fin to attract prey.&nbsp;
Even deeper is the abyssopelagic zone,&nbsp;
extending from 4000 to 6000 meters.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Temperatures in the abyssopelagic zone&nbsp;
stay at a chilly 4 degrees Celsius, and&nbsp;&nbsp;
pressures reach 200 to 600 times the atmospheric&nbsp;
pressure at sea level. Incredibly, there are some&nbsp;&nbsp;
organisms that can withstand these conditions.&nbsp;
Sea spiders with spindly, 30-centimeter-long legs&nbsp;&nbsp;
and mud-eating sea pigs scuttle across the bottom&nbsp;
of the ocean floor, making an unlikely home.&nbsp;
Finally, the last zone is the hadopelagic&nbsp;
zone, and it extends from 6000 to as far&nbsp;&nbsp;
as 11,000 meters. The hadopelagic zone&nbsp;
is only found in deep ocean trenches,&nbsp;&nbsp;
like the Mariana, and it is occupied only by&nbsp;
a few species of tube worms and sea cucumbers.&nbsp;
Now it is certainly the case that we could talk&nbsp;
much more extensively about all of the fascinating&nbsp;&nbsp;
living organisms that inhabit each of these zones.&nbsp;
But that is the subject of the zoology series,&nbsp;&nbsp;

animal life, be sure to visit that playlist&nbsp;&nbsp;

geology series, it is merely important to have&nbsp;&nbsp;
a basic understanding of the layers of the ocean&nbsp;
and the depths they correspond with, as well as&nbsp;&nbsp;
the general features of each layer. After all, the&nbsp;
ocean is what sits atop the oceanic crust, filling&nbsp;&nbsp;
in every nook and cranny, from the deepest trench&nbsp;
all the way up to sea level. And with the ocean&nbsp;&nbsp;
