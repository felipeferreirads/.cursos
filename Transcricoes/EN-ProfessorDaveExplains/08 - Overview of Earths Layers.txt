Now that we’ve discussed the stresses that affect&nbsp;
Earth’s rocks, let’s go into detail about the&nbsp;&nbsp;
different layers of Earth. On the surface, Earth&nbsp;
may seem like a relatively stable place, with the&nbsp;&nbsp;
occasional earthquake or volcanic eruption, but&nbsp;
our experiences are limited to the uppermost part&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Earth, the crust. Earth’s crust comes in two&nbsp;
varieties: continental and oceanic. Continental&nbsp;&nbsp;
crust is felsic, or rich in silica, and is around&nbsp;
20 to 70 kilometers thick. Oceanic crust is mafic,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or rich in iron and magnesium, and is around 5 to&nbsp;
10 kilometers thick. However, the most important&nbsp;&nbsp;
difference between oceanic and continental crust&nbsp;
is their density. Oceanic crust is more dense&nbsp;&nbsp;
than continental crust, therefore when a piece&nbsp;
of oceanic crust collides with continental crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the oceanic crust slides beneath the continental&nbsp;
crust in a process that we’ve mentioned,&nbsp;&nbsp;
called subduction. The subducted oceanic crust&nbsp;
will then sink deeper and deeper into the mantle&nbsp;&nbsp;
until eventually reaching the bottom of&nbsp;
the mantle. Oceanic crust is constantly&nbsp;&nbsp;
being subducted and therefore destroyed. There&nbsp;
are large areas of ancient, 4-billion-year-old&nbsp;&nbsp;
continental crust called cratons, but the oldest&nbsp;
oceanic crust is only 340 million years old.&nbsp;
Below the crust is the mantle, a solid, but&nbsp;
malleable layer with temperatures reaching&nbsp;&nbsp;
3500 degrees Celsius and pressures up to a million&nbsp;
times greater than atmospheric pressure. Although&nbsp;&nbsp;
the mantle is solid, over millions of years,&nbsp;
it convects like the world’s largest lava lamp,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as earth’s primordial and radiogenic heat is&nbsp;
transferred to the surface and radiated to space.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Mid-ocean ridges and rift zones&nbsp;
form beneath large areas of hot,&nbsp;&nbsp;
upwelling mantle, while subduction zones&nbsp;
form beneath areas of cold, sinking mantle.&nbsp;&nbsp;
When subducting oceanic crust sinks into&nbsp;
the mantle, it creates a suction-like force&nbsp;&nbsp;
in its wake, which pulls the subducting slab&nbsp;
further into the mantle. This slab-pull force&nbsp;&nbsp;
is driven by buoyancy, due to oceanic crust&nbsp;
becoming more dense with age. Eventually it&nbsp;&nbsp;
becomes more dense than the mantle beneath it,&nbsp;
which is when subduction can begin. According&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the current scientific consensus, slab pull&nbsp;
is the main force that causes plate tectonics.&nbsp;
The mantle extends from 70 to 2900 kilometers&nbsp;
below the surface and is mainly composed of an&nbsp;&nbsp;
ultramafic rock called peridotite, which is mainly&nbsp;
composed of the mineral olivine, or MgFeSiO4.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Geologists have found xenoliths, or strange rocks,&nbsp;
of peridotite in massive volcanic eruptions.&nbsp;&nbsp;
By analyzing the minerals in peridotite,&nbsp;
scientists can determine what depth the peridotite&nbsp;&nbsp;
came from. Diamonds, for example, are formed&nbsp;
at depths of about 150 kilometers or deeper,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and are primarily found in another&nbsp;
ultramafic xenolith called kimberlite.&nbsp;
At the bottom of the mantle lies a very&nbsp;
mysterious, but geologically important&nbsp;&nbsp;
layer called D’’. This layer is marked by a large&nbsp;
thermal gradient and an increased heterogeneity.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The exact nature of this layer is unknown, but&nbsp;
there are some hypotheses. Some believe that&nbsp;&nbsp;
the D’’ layer is the “slab graveyard”, or the&nbsp;
place where subducted slabs eventually settle.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Some believe that mantle plumes, or cylindrical&nbsp;
areas of hot, rising mantle, initiate from within&nbsp;&nbsp;
D’’ in areas where there is increased heat flux&nbsp;
from the core. Mantle plumes have caused some&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the largest volcanoes in Earth’s History,&nbsp;
with modern examples being located in Hawaii,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Iceland, and Yellowstone. The accumulation of&nbsp;
subducted slabs at D’’ also has implications&nbsp;&nbsp;
for triggering magnetic field reversals; but&nbsp;
before we get there we must discuss the core.&nbsp;
At 2900 kilometers, the D’’ layer gives way to&nbsp;
a liquid outer core composed mainly of iron and&nbsp;&nbsp;
nickel. Here, temperatures reach 5500 degrees&nbsp;
Celsius, and pressures exceed 300 million times&nbsp;&nbsp;
the atmospheric pressure on the surface. Since&nbsp;
this layer is a liquid, it can easily convect,&nbsp;&nbsp;
carrying heat from the inner core into the&nbsp;
mantle through the D’’ layer. The rotation&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Earth on its axis further modifies the&nbsp;
flow and creates large, cylindrical helices.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Since iron is an electrically conductive material,&nbsp;
when it swirls through Earth’s magnetic field it&nbsp;&nbsp;
creates an electric field, which in turn creates&nbsp;
an additional magnetic field, strengthening the&nbsp;&nbsp;
original field. This is called the geodynamo,&nbsp;
and it is how earth maintains its magnetic field.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The geodynamo does require a “seed” magnetic&nbsp;
field to get going, which was likely provided&nbsp;&nbsp;
during the T-Tauri stage of the early sun.
At 5100 kilometers, the pressure becomes so great&nbsp;&nbsp;
that iron is forced into the solid phase. This&nbsp;
boundary is called the Bullen discontinuity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and it marks the division between the liquid&nbsp;
outer core and the dense, solid inner core. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
inner core is about the size of the moon and has a&nbsp;
temperature equivalent to the surface of the sun.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Though the inner core does not generate a magnetic&nbsp;
field, it does help lock-in the field lines and&nbsp;&nbsp;
stabilize the magnetic field generated in&nbsp;
the outer core. The inner core also rotates&nbsp;&nbsp;
faster than Earth as a whole. This phenomenon is&nbsp;
called super-rotation. It is thought to be caused&nbsp;&nbsp;
by rotating magnetic fields in the outer core&nbsp;
exerting a magnetic torque on the inner core,&nbsp;&nbsp;
similar to how an induction motor works. Magnetic&nbsp;
fields induce electric fields in conductors,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the created electric field manifests an&nbsp;
additional magnetic field that interacts with&nbsp;&nbsp;
the original magnetic field. In an electric&nbsp;
motor, the stator windings produce a rotating&nbsp;&nbsp;
magnetic field, and the rotating magnetic&nbsp;
field induces an electric current in the rotor,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which induces a secondary magnetic field that&nbsp;
interacts with the original rotating field causing&nbsp;&nbsp;
the rotor to spin, as it is “dragged” along&nbsp;
by the rotating magnetic field of the stator.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Earth’s magnetic field undergoes periodic&nbsp;
reversals where the field strength drops to&nbsp;&nbsp;
near zero, and the poles switch. Failed reversals&nbsp;
also occur, where the field strength drops to&nbsp;&nbsp;
near zero and the original field eventually&nbsp;
reestablishes. The last reversal was around&nbsp;&nbsp;
780,000 years ago and we may be due for one soon.&nbsp;
It is not known what triggers magnetic reversals.&nbsp;&nbsp;
One hypothesis is the sudden collapse of&nbsp;
large amounts of cold, subducted slabs&nbsp;&nbsp;
into the D’’ layer, which can reorganize outer&nbsp;
core convection, altering the magnetic field.&nbsp;
Now that we know all about the Earth’s layers,&nbsp;
lets discuss how they formed. When Earth first&nbsp;&nbsp;
accreted, it was an extremely hot, homogeneous&nbsp;
planet, since the material present was primarily&nbsp;&nbsp;
chondrite meteorites. At some point early in its&nbsp;
history, the combined heat of radioactive decay&nbsp;&nbsp;
and kinetic energy of the accretion process caused&nbsp;
Earth to melt. The process of differentiation was&nbsp;&nbsp;
then able to begin, with the heaviest materials&nbsp;
like iron and nickel, sinking toward the center&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the Earth, and the lightest materials, like&nbsp;
silicon and oxygen, rising toward the surface.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Eventually, earth cooled enough to where the&nbsp;
crust and mantle crystallized, with the core&nbsp;&nbsp;
remaining entirely molten for a long period of&nbsp;
time. The start of crystallization of the inner&nbsp;&nbsp;
core is debated, with estimates ranging from about&nbsp;
2 billion to 570 million years ago. Earth has been&nbsp;&nbsp;
cooling, radiating its heat to space, since&nbsp;
its formation. At the moment, the inner core&nbsp;&nbsp;
is growing at a rate of a millimeter per year&nbsp;
as the liquid iron in the outer core solidifies,&nbsp;&nbsp;
forming new crystals of solid iron. If&nbsp;
Earth were to exist in 91 billion years,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Earth would have an entirely solid iron core,&nbsp;
and Earth’s magnetic field would be gone. However,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it’s likely that the sun will engulf the Earth&nbsp;
in a red giant phase long before then. A poetic&nbsp;&nbsp;
end to our hero’s journey, and an amazing sight&nbsp;
for any sentient beings that were able to escape&nbsp;&nbsp;
the same fate, by venturing into the cosmos.
