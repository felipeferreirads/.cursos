With fluvial and aeolian weathering environments&nbsp;
covered, we have discussed flowing water and wind,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the weathering they can produce. Let’s finish&nbsp;
up by talking about ice. Glaciers are massive&nbsp;&nbsp;
bodies of long-lasting, older than a decade ice&nbsp;
that can move under their own weight. Over 60%&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Earth’s freshwater is tied up in glaciers, but&nbsp;
global climate change has caused glaciers to melt&nbsp;&nbsp;
at nearly twice the rate they were melting just&nbsp;
20 years ago. Glaciers form on mountainous peaks&nbsp;&nbsp;
and at high latitudes, where the climate is cool&nbsp;
enough to support year-long snow cover. Glaciers&nbsp;&nbsp;
are truly just giant piles of snow that compressed&nbsp;
under their own weight into crystalline ice, and&nbsp;&nbsp;
ice sheets are enormous glaciers that form over&nbsp;
entire continents during times of global cooling.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Both glaciers and ice sheets often have&nbsp;
a layer of liquid water beneath them,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where the ice would meet bedrock. This liquid&nbsp;
layer reduces friction and allows the ice sheet&nbsp;&nbsp;
to slip along the bedrock surface, where it&nbsp;
rips up and plows Earth’s surface like a giant&nbsp;&nbsp;
bulldozer. In the absence of a liquid layer,&nbsp;
ice sheets deform plastically within the ice&nbsp;&nbsp;
itself and do not slip along the surface,&nbsp;
therefore not altering the landscape much.&nbsp;
By about 2.6 million years ago, the Laurentide&nbsp;
Ice sheet covered most of Canada and the northern&nbsp;&nbsp;
third of the Eastern and Central United&nbsp;
States, which began to melt and retreat&nbsp;&nbsp;
around 11,600 years ago. Though the ice sheet&nbsp;
existed throughout this period, it would expand&nbsp;&nbsp;
and contract periodically in response to global&nbsp;
climate. During the expanse of an ice sheet, it&nbsp;&nbsp;
is mainly erosional, ripping up pieces of bedrock&nbsp;
below, absorbing them into its frozen mass,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and transporting them down flow. When an ice sheet&nbsp;
expands, it scrapes the area down to the bedrock,&nbsp;&nbsp;
entraining sediments from silt to huge, angular&nbsp;
boulders. In areas where silt lies between the&nbsp;&nbsp;
ice and bedrock, the pressure of the ice and&nbsp;
movement of the glacier act like a giant polisher,&nbsp;&nbsp;
creating a smooth surface called a slickenside.&nbsp;
However, when cobbles or boulders are stuck&nbsp;&nbsp;
between the ice and bedrock, the bedrock is&nbsp;
gouged, or scratched, creating grooves that&nbsp;&nbsp;
geologists call slickenlines, which are aligned&nbsp;
parallel to the motion of the glacier, making&nbsp;&nbsp;
them useful for studying past, extinct glaciers.
There are many types of glacial sediment that&nbsp;&nbsp;
are all generally referred to as glacial till.&nbsp;
But there are two main types of glacial till,&nbsp;&nbsp;
basal till, which encompasses the entrained&nbsp;
sediment at the bottom of the glacier,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and ablation till, which refers to sediment that&nbsp;
was transported near or at the top of the glacier&nbsp;&nbsp;
and later deposited when the ice melted.&nbsp;
Not only can basal till be carried by ice,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it can also be carried by subglacial&nbsp;
streams, which can be either beneath the glacier,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or on its sides along the valley walls. Till&nbsp;
that is modified or deposited by these subglacial&nbsp;&nbsp;
streams gets sorted and rounded by the meltwater&nbsp;
and is called ice contact stratified drift.&nbsp;
But what about ablation till, how can&nbsp;
sediment be on top of a glacier? Well,&nbsp;&nbsp;
this occurs when a glacier is flowing through&nbsp;
a valley where the top of the ice is lower than&nbsp;&nbsp;
the valley walls. In such a case, sediment can&nbsp;
then fall on top of the glacier during collapses&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the valley walls. Since this sediment simply&nbsp;
falls on top of the ice and remains there until&nbsp;&nbsp;
the ice melts, it is not really modified by its&nbsp;
environment, as with rounding due to abrasion,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and it is therefore very angular.
Most glacial till is deposited once&nbsp;&nbsp;
it starts to retreat and melt. Moraines are&nbsp;
large piles of poorly sorted sediments that&nbsp;&nbsp;
pile up around the ice sheet as it melts. End&nbsp;
moraines form in front of a retreating glacier,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and lateral moraines form along its sides.&nbsp;
Moraines are kind of like the pile of debris&nbsp;&nbsp;
in front of an advancing bulldozer, though in&nbsp;
actuality, they can form in a variety of ways.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Proglacial lakes can form between a retreating&nbsp;
glacier and an end moraine, where large deposits&nbsp;&nbsp;
of clay and silt accumulate. Glacial lakes&nbsp;
can also form in north-draining valleys,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where the drainage is dammed by the ice&nbsp;
sheet itself. This was the case for the&nbsp;&nbsp;
great Lake Missoula, which existed in western&nbsp;
Montana toward the end of the last ice age.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Lake Missoula’s ice dam would periodically&nbsp;
fail, causing cataclysmic floods which produced&nbsp;&nbsp;
gigantic dunes up to 50 feet high, due to water&nbsp;
velocities of up to 130 kilometers per hour.&nbsp;
When ice sheets melt, they produce tremendous&nbsp;
volumes of water with high flow velocities.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Large melting events can carry huge&nbsp;
amounts of coarse and poorly sorted&nbsp;&nbsp;
sediment away from the retreating ice&nbsp;
sheet, producing laterally extensive,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sheet-like deposits of well-rounded boulders,&nbsp;
cobbles, sand, and silt called glacial outwash.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In stream valleys, glacial outwash deposits can be&nbsp;
several hundred feet thick. This type of sediment&nbsp;&nbsp;
is extremely well-drained and makes great aquifers&nbsp;
due to its high permeability. Glacial sediments&nbsp;&nbsp;
generally make poor soils since they are hard&nbsp;
and compacted, poorly chemically weathered, and&nbsp;&nbsp;
composed primarily of unsorted, rocky sediments.&nbsp;
Chemical weathering helps make a good soil,&nbsp;&nbsp;
since it helps soften and break up rocks, and&nbsp;
leaches important nutrients from minerals,&nbsp;&nbsp;
incorporating them into soil pore waters, and&nbsp;
since glacial sediment is young and exists in&nbsp;&nbsp;
cold environments, it is not very weathered. Of&nbsp;
course, there can be too much of a good thing,&nbsp;&nbsp;
since many tropical soils have had all their&nbsp;
nutrients removed, leaving behind mainly quartz&nbsp;&nbsp;
and aluminum hydroxides. There are some&nbsp;
glacial deposits that make fertile soil;&nbsp;&nbsp;
one of them is called loess, which is wind-blown&nbsp;
silt that accumulates downwind of the ice sheet.&nbsp;
With that we have covered a wide variety of&nbsp;
weathering processes and environments, from&nbsp;&nbsp;
water, to air, to ice, so we should now have a&nbsp;
reasonable understanding of sedimentary processes.
