In the previous tutorial we introduced the&nbsp;
figures involved in the early development&nbsp;&nbsp;
of plate tectonics. Let’s now talk&nbsp;
about how plate tectonics has expanded&nbsp;&nbsp;
into a global-scale model for the formation&nbsp;
and destruction of supercontinents called the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Wilson Cycle. The Wilson Cycle, named for Canadian&nbsp;
geologist, J. Tuzo Wilson, describes the rifting,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or breaking apart of continents, the subsequent&nbsp;
opening of new ocean basins, and the eventual&nbsp;&nbsp;
closing of these basins via subduction.&nbsp;
We will start our discussion of the Wilson&nbsp;&nbsp;
Cycle with the first stage: the embryonic stage.
Let’s set the stage for discussing a Wilson Cycle&nbsp;&nbsp;
using the breakup of Pangea and opening of&nbsp;
the Atlantic Ocean as an example. About 300&nbsp;&nbsp;
million years ago, all of Earth’s continents were&nbsp;
collected into one landmass, the supercontinent&nbsp;&nbsp;
Pangea, which existed for over 100 million years.&nbsp;
There have been at least three supercontinents in&nbsp;&nbsp;
Earth’s history, all of which succumbed to the&nbsp;
Wilson Cycle. The embryonic stage of the Wilson&nbsp;&nbsp;
Cycle begins with the initiation of rifting&nbsp;
on the supercontinent, in this case Pangea.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Unfortunately, the rifting of supercontinents&nbsp;
is poorly understood and can theoretically be&nbsp;&nbsp;
explained by a few different mechanisms. These&nbsp;
explanations fall under two main categories,&nbsp;&nbsp;
active and passive rifting. Active rifting is&nbsp;
relatively straightforward and is caused by&nbsp;&nbsp;
a convection-driven mantle plume beneath the&nbsp;
supercontinent, which heats and pushes upward&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the lithosphere, stretching it like&nbsp;
taffy until it eventually breaks apart.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Rifting continents typically break apart&nbsp;
along three-line segments in a Y-shape,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the center of the Y lying beneath the&nbsp;
mantle plume, which is called a triple junction.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Red Sea and East African Rift is an&nbsp;
excellent example of a modern triple junction.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The other category of rifting is&nbsp;
passive rifting, passive meaning&nbsp;&nbsp;
that convection of the mantle is not causal&nbsp;
to rifting. In this model, tensional forces,&nbsp;&nbsp;
whether gravitational or tectonic, cause extension&nbsp;
of the lithosphere, and as the lithosphere thins,&nbsp;&nbsp;
hot asthenosphere flows upward to fill the&nbsp;
void, initiating formation of a mantle plume.&nbsp;&nbsp;
No matter what the cause, Pangea began&nbsp;
experiencing rifting around 180 million years ago.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Rifting is always accompanied by the formation&nbsp;
of large normal faults and extensional fractures,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which guide large volumes of molten asthenosphere&nbsp;
to the surface, forming fissure volcanoes,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or linear volcanic vents. Eventually, the&nbsp;
lithosphere is thinned and fractured so much that&nbsp;&nbsp;
it is separated into three pieces about the triple&nbsp;
junction, creating three new tectonic plates.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Above the rift, surface elevation drops as&nbsp;
the crust thins, and deep, elongated lakes&nbsp;&nbsp;
begin to form, widening and deepening with time.&nbsp;
Lake Victoria, Lake Malawi, and Lake Tanganyika&nbsp;&nbsp;
are all examples of rift valley lakes in&nbsp;
the East African Rift System. Eventually,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the rift valleys become so wide that they connect&nbsp;
to the ocean, creating narrow, saltwater gulfs,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and a passive margin, or tectonically inactive&nbsp;
area, is developed along new shoreline.&nbsp;
This is the beginning of the juvenile stage of&nbsp;
the Wilson Cycle. Modern examples of locations&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the juvenile stage are the Red Sea,&nbsp;
separating Africa and Saudi Arabia,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the Sea of Cortes that separates Baja, Mexico&nbsp;
from mainland Mexico. At this stage, the crust&nbsp;&nbsp;
overlying the mantle plume has been stretched so&nbsp;
thin, that some of the hot, mantle material begins&nbsp;&nbsp;
to reach the surface where it erupts from volcanic&nbsp;
fissures along the active rift, and new oceanic&nbsp;&nbsp;
crust is created at this juvenile mid-ocean ridge,&nbsp;
forming a divergent boundary. Divergent boundaries&nbsp;&nbsp;
are regions where tectonic plates are moving away&nbsp;
from each other. Around 180 million years ago,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Atlantic Ocean was just a nascent rift valley&nbsp;
in Pangea. Over millions of years, that rift&nbsp;&nbsp;
valley grew, and its volcanoes gushed, generating&nbsp;
over 40 million square miles of new oceanic crust.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Today, the Atlantic Ocean spans 3000 miles at its&nbsp;
widest, and it continues to grow by one and a half&nbsp;&nbsp;
inches each year along the mid-Atlantic ridge.&nbsp;
This is the mature stage of the Wilson Cycle,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is distinguished by the&nbsp;
formation of a vast ocean basin.&nbsp;
During the next stage, the declining stage,&nbsp;
the aged ocean basin begins to close,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and we begin moving toward the formation of the&nbsp;
next supercontinent, which is accomplished through&nbsp;&nbsp;
subduction of the oceanic crust. But why does&nbsp;
subduction begin? In order to understand this,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we need to examine how oceanic lithosphere&nbsp;
changes with time. The lithosphere has two parts,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the crust, and the lithospheric mantle. Below&nbsp;
the lithospheric mantle is the asthenosphere.&nbsp;
When oceanic lithosphere first forms, it is very&nbsp;
hot and its lithospheric mantle is extremely thin.&nbsp;&nbsp;
But the plate will steadily inch away from&nbsp;
the mid-ocean ridge which allows the oceanic&nbsp;&nbsp;
lithosphere to slowly cool and thicken. As this&nbsp;
layer grows, the oceanic lithosphere becomes&nbsp;&nbsp;
more and more dense, until it eventually begins&nbsp;
to sink into the ductile asthenosphere below.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This is subduction. Typically, oceanic&nbsp;
lithosphere will begin to subduct after&nbsp;&nbsp;
it reaches an age of around 200 million&nbsp;
years. So to put it simply, subduction&nbsp;&nbsp;
occurs when oceanic crust is old and cold.
Subduction zones are convergent plate boundaries&nbsp;&nbsp;
where two tectonic plates are colliding. They come&nbsp;
in two types: ocean-ocean or ocean-continental.&nbsp;&nbsp;
At an ocean-ocean convergent boundary, old,&nbsp;
dense oceanic crust subducts underneath the&nbsp;&nbsp;
younger, more buoyant oceanic crust of&nbsp;
the overlying tectonic plate. This type of&nbsp;&nbsp;
boundary results in the formation of volcanic&nbsp;
island arcs, such as the Aleutian Islands.&nbsp;&nbsp;
During subduction, water is delivered into the&nbsp;
mantle via the dehydration of hydroxyl-bearing&nbsp;&nbsp;
minerals within the subducted oceanic lithosphere,&nbsp;
which lowers the melting point of the mantle rock,&nbsp;&nbsp;
causing widespread volcanism. At an&nbsp;
ocean-continental boundary, dense oceanic crust&nbsp;&nbsp;
subducts beneath continental crust from the&nbsp;
overlying tectonic plate, which due to its low&nbsp;&nbsp;
density, cannot subduct. During subduction,&nbsp;
the sediment that has settled on top of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
oceanic crust is scraped off onto the overlying&nbsp;
continental crust, creating a feature called an&nbsp;&nbsp;
accretionary wedge and subduction-related&nbsp;
magmatism creates a chain of volcanoes,&nbsp;&nbsp;
called a continental arc. The Cascade Range&nbsp;
of the Pacific Northwest is an example of&nbsp;&nbsp;
such an arc. Here, the Juan de Fuca plate is&nbsp;
subducting beneath the North American plate.&nbsp;
After a few hundred million years, subduction&nbsp;
has nearly consumed all oceanic crust&nbsp;&nbsp;
as the old fragments of rifted supercontinent&nbsp;
move closer and closer together, to eventually&nbsp;&nbsp;
be joined once again. The collision and&nbsp;
suturing of the pieces of rifted supercontinent&nbsp;&nbsp;
marks the terminal stage of the Wilson Cycle&nbsp;
and a brand-new supercontinent is formed.&nbsp;&nbsp;
During the end-stage of subduction, the last&nbsp;
piece of the subducting oceanic lithosphere&nbsp;&nbsp;
acts as a ramp, leading one continent to be thrust&nbsp;
up and over the other. This stitching together of&nbsp;&nbsp;
two pieces of continental crust is the final&nbsp;
stage of the Wilson Cycle: the suturing stage;&nbsp;&nbsp;
during which a new massive continental&nbsp;
craton forms and the Wilson cycle resets.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The boundary between two colliding plates of&nbsp;
continental crust is called a continent-continent&nbsp;&nbsp;
convergent boundary. Since continental&nbsp;
crust is so buoyant, neither plate subducts.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Instead, both plates continue to collide,&nbsp;
forcing the crust upward into dramatic&nbsp;&nbsp;
mountain ranges. The collision process causes&nbsp;
the rocks here to be intensively folded,&nbsp;&nbsp;
faulted, and recrystallized. The Himalayas are&nbsp;
one example of a continent-continent convergent&nbsp;&nbsp;
boundary formed by the collision of the Indian and&nbsp;
Eurasian Plates, and a testament to the awesome&nbsp;&nbsp;
power of this geological process. And with that,&nbsp;
we have a reasonable understanding of the Wilson&nbsp;&nbsp;
cycle and its timeline, as well as the stunning&nbsp;
geological features it is responsible for.
