In the previous tutorial we learned about how&nbsp;
rocks melt, differentiate, and crystallize.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Now we will discuss the different types of&nbsp;
igneous rocks and how they are classified.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Say you are given an igneous rock and asked what&nbsp;
it is. Where would you begin? Your first step&nbsp;&nbsp;
might be determining whether it was extrusive&nbsp;
or intrusive. Intrusive is a word describing&nbsp;&nbsp;
an igneous rock that cooled slowly at depth,&nbsp;
and extrusive is a word describing a rock that&nbsp;&nbsp;
cooled quickly at the surface, usually during a&nbsp;
volcanic eruption. Intrusive rocks are also called&nbsp;&nbsp;
plutonic, and extrusive rocks may be described as&nbsp;
volcanic. It is usually pretty easy to distinguish&nbsp;&nbsp;
a plutonic rock from a volcanic rock simply by&nbsp;
analyzing the size of the mineral grains. Plutonic&nbsp;&nbsp;
rocks, which cool slowly, have ample time to&nbsp;
grow large crystals, while volcanic rocks, which&nbsp;&nbsp;
crystallize rapidly, are composed of tiny grains&nbsp;
that usually can’t be seen by the naked eye.&nbsp;
Let’s first discuss volcanic rocks,&nbsp;
since they are simply named after the&nbsp;&nbsp;
magma from which they are derived. Both are&nbsp;
classified based on their silica content,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and are as follows: with rhyolite, silica&nbsp;
content is greater than 69%, dacite, 62-69%,&nbsp;&nbsp;
andesite, 52-62%, basalt, 45-52%, and picrite,&nbsp;
which contains less than 45% silica. The term&nbsp;&nbsp;
ultramafic is commonly used to describe picritic&nbsp;
rocks, mafic for basaltic rocks, intermediate for&nbsp;&nbsp;
andesitic rocks, and felsic for dacitic and&nbsp;
rhyolitic rocks. Rhyolite and basalt are very&nbsp;&nbsp;
different rocks, and it is typical for them not to&nbsp;
have any common minerals. For example, rhyolite is&nbsp;&nbsp;
rich in alkali feldspar, quartz, and sodium-rich&nbsp;
plagioclase with minor amounts of amphibole,&nbsp;&nbsp;
while basalt is mainly composed of pyroxene,&nbsp;
olivine, and calcium-rich plagioclase with minor&nbsp;&nbsp;
amounts of iron and titanium oxides. Andesites&nbsp;
and dacites can have both felsic and mafic&nbsp;&nbsp;
minerals but are typically mostly plagioclase with&nbsp;
lesser amounts of pyroxene, amphibole, and quartz.&nbsp;
Plutonic igneous rocks have an entirely different&nbsp;
naming convention than volcanic rocks, which are&nbsp;&nbsp;
classified based on their composition; instead&nbsp;
being named based on their three most abundant&nbsp;&nbsp;
minerals. This is because the minerals in plutonic&nbsp;
rocks can be easily identified in hand sample,&nbsp;&nbsp;
whereas volcanic minerals are typically&nbsp;
too small. For felsic plutonic rocks,&nbsp;&nbsp;
names are based on the relative amounts of&nbsp;
quartz, plagioclase, and alkali feldspar,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are placed at the corners of a triangular,&nbsp;
or ternary diagram, called a QAP diagram.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Plagioclase-rich rocks are called diorite,&nbsp;
quartz-rich rocks are called granitoid,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and alkali feldspar-rich rocks are called syenite.&nbsp;
Rocks of intermediate composition are called&nbsp;&nbsp;
granite if they are quartz-rich and monzonite if&nbsp;
they are feldspar-rich. On the other hand, mafic&nbsp;&nbsp;
plutonic rocks are named based on their relative&nbsp;
abundances of olivine, pyroxene, and plagioclase,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and again are plotted on ternary diagrams. Gabbro,&nbsp;
which is the plutonic version of basalt, contains&nbsp;&nbsp;
roughly equal parts plagioclase and clinopyroxene.&nbsp;
Likewise, ultramafic rocks can be classified&nbsp;&nbsp;
using the minerals olivine, clinopyroxene, and&nbsp;
orthopyroxene. These rocks, like harzburgite and&nbsp;&nbsp;
lherzolite, are representative of the upper&nbsp;
mantle and are rarely found at the surface.&nbsp;
So, what types of igneous rocks are found at&nbsp;
the surface? That depends on where you look.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For instance, the lava that erupts from midocean&nbsp;
ridges is exclusively basaltic, with gabbro&nbsp;&nbsp;
forming deeper beneath the surface, which is&nbsp;
basically just basalt with big crystals. Hotspots&nbsp;&nbsp;
are also capable of producing massive amounts of&nbsp;
basaltic lava, especially when they are beneath&nbsp;&nbsp;
oceanic crust. Subduction zones tend to produce&nbsp;
andesitic magma, but can be much more variable,&nbsp;&nbsp;
also being influenced by crustal thickness.&nbsp;
Island arcs, which are subduction zones where the&nbsp;&nbsp;
overriding plate is oceanic crust, tend to produce&nbsp;
basaltic and andesitic magma, while continental&nbsp;&nbsp;
arcs, like the volcanoes of the Pacific Northwest,&nbsp;
tend to erupt rhyolite and dacite. With igneous&nbsp;&nbsp;
rocks now covered, let’s move forward and check&nbsp;
out some other types of rocks and minerals.
