We just learned about how to identify minerals on&nbsp;
the basis of luster and color. There are two more&nbsp;&nbsp;
important characteristics we will also need to&nbsp;
discuss. One of the best ways to identify minerals&nbsp;&nbsp;
is by examining their cleavage, or lack thereof.&nbsp;
A mineral is said to have cleavage if it breaks&nbsp;&nbsp;
along a planar surface in one or more directions.&nbsp;
The way that a mineral breaks is fundamentally&nbsp;&nbsp;
controlled by its crystalline structure, and more&nbsp;
specifically its relative strength along different&nbsp;&nbsp;
crystallographic directions. For example, graphite&nbsp;
is composed of stacks of two-dimensional sheets of&nbsp;&nbsp;
carbon atoms bonded together through van der Waals&nbsp;
forces. The carbon atoms themselves are strongly&nbsp;&nbsp;
bonded within a sheet, but the bonds between the&nbsp;
sheets are extremely weak. Because of this, when&nbsp;&nbsp;
graphite is broken, it breaks into planar sheets&nbsp;
along the direction of the weak van der Waals&nbsp;&nbsp;
bonds, but breaks irregularly along the direction&nbsp;
of the stronger, more isotropic covalent bonds.&nbsp;&nbsp;
A mineral that has one direction of cleavage,&nbsp;
like graphite, is said to have basal cleavage.&nbsp;&nbsp;
A class of minerals called phyllosilicates,&nbsp;
or sheet silicates, are easily identified&nbsp;&nbsp;
based on their perfect basal cleavage, causing&nbsp;
them to break apart like the crust of baklava.&nbsp;
Some minerals totally lack cleavage and break&nbsp;
along random, uneven fractures. The mineral&nbsp;&nbsp;
quartz, or silicon dioxide, has a lattice composed&nbsp;
of equal-strength silicon-oxygen bonds in all&nbsp;&nbsp;
directions, and therefore lacks cleavage. However,&nbsp;
quartz does have a characteristic fracture pattern&nbsp;&nbsp;
called conchoidal fracture. A conchoidal&nbsp;
fracture appears as a concave “scoop” with&nbsp;&nbsp;
a series of parallel arc-like steps along its&nbsp;
surface. Think of a broken piece of thick glass,&nbsp;&nbsp;
like the bottom of a glass soda bottle. When&nbsp;
a mineral has more than one plane of cleavage,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it is useful to estimate the angle between them,&nbsp;
which can also be diagnostic. The minerals quartz,&nbsp;&nbsp;
calcite, and halite often appear very similar in&nbsp;
color and luster. The easiest way to tell them&nbsp;&nbsp;
apart, is to look for cleavage. Quartz has no&nbsp;
cleavage, whereas calcite has 3 directions of&nbsp;&nbsp;
cleavage. In addition, while both halite and&nbsp;
calcite have three directions of cleavage,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the cleavage planes of calcite are 60- and&nbsp;
120-degrees apart vs. 90 degrees for halite. If&nbsp;&nbsp;
you were to smash halite with a hammer, it would&nbsp;
break into tiny cubes. Do the same to calcite&nbsp;&nbsp;
and it would break into tiny rhombohedrons. Two&nbsp;
of the most common minerals in Earth’s crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
quartz and plagioclase, can also look similar in&nbsp;
color and luster, especially to the untrained eye.&nbsp;&nbsp;
But plagioclase has two directions of cleavage at&nbsp;
90 degrees in contrast with quartz’s lack thereof.&nbsp;
Even scratching a rock with your fingernail can&nbsp;
be a quick way to classify and identify minerals,&nbsp;&nbsp;
telling you something about the strength of its&nbsp;
chemical bonds. A typical human fingernail has a&nbsp;&nbsp;
hardness, or resistance to scratching, similar&nbsp;
to the mineral calcite. Austrian mineralogist,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Friedrich Mohs, determined this literal rule of&nbsp;
thumb while developing the Mohs Hardness Scale.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Mohs scale ranks minerals by their ability&nbsp;
to scratch each other, and is used to measure&nbsp;&nbsp;
the relative hardness of a mineral sample.&nbsp;
The scale goes from 1, the softest mineral,&nbsp;&nbsp;
talc, to 10, the hardest mineral, diamond.&nbsp;
Geologists also use common objects like coins or a&nbsp;&nbsp;
metal fingernail file to approximate the hardness&nbsp;
of minerals. Pennies have a hardness of about 3.5&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the Mohs scale, similar to fluorite, and metal&nbsp;
files have a hardness of 6.5 on the Mohs scale,&nbsp;&nbsp;
similar to quartz. For example, say you have an&nbsp;
unknown mineral that can be scratched by a steel&nbsp;&nbsp;
file, but not by a penny. This would indicate that&nbsp;
your mineral has a hardness between 3.5 and 6.5.&nbsp;
So that wraps up a discussion surrounding some&nbsp;
of the physical characteristics of minerals.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Now that we’ve related the atomic structure&nbsp;
of minerals to their macroscopic properties,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we can move on to the&nbsp;
different classes of minerals&nbsp;&nbsp;
and discover how crystal chemistry and&nbsp;
crystal structure relate to one another.
