In the previous tutorial we discussed the&nbsp;
types of sediment that cover oceanic crust.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Now it’s on to the crust itself. Beneath the&nbsp;
sediment is a layer of rock called pillow lava.&nbsp;&nbsp;
At mid-ocean ridges where new oceanic crust is&nbsp;
made, molten lava erupts from cracks in the crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where it almost instantly cools once in contact&nbsp;
with the cold ocean water. The rapidly quenched&nbsp;&nbsp;
lava forms pillows or bulbous bodies which&nbsp;
can then break open and exude more hot lava&nbsp;&nbsp;
from beneath the surface to form additional&nbsp;
pillows. This process repeats over and over,&nbsp;&nbsp;
forming a thick layer of stacked pillow lavas.&nbsp;
The pillow lavas are often called pillow basalts,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the term basalt referring to the lava’s&nbsp;
mafic composition and fine grain size. Basaltic&nbsp;&nbsp;
rocks typically lack visible crystals.
Underneath the pillow basalt is a layer&nbsp;&nbsp;
of rock called sheeted dikes. Dikes are a sort of&nbsp;
underground plumbing system that bring magma from&nbsp;&nbsp;
the mantle upward, toward the ocean floor. They&nbsp;
are essentially fractures caused by extensional&nbsp;&nbsp;
faulting that act as magma conduits. Sheeted dikes&nbsp;
are formed when the rising magma crystallizes&nbsp;&nbsp;
within the fracture-conduits, instead of extruding&nbsp;
from the ocean floor to form pillow lava, and over&nbsp;&nbsp;
time, clusters of basalt columns form, creating&nbsp;
a sheeted dike complex that looks like a row of&nbsp;&nbsp;
several-meter-long dominos standing side-by-side.
Beneath the sheeted dike complex is a layer of&nbsp;&nbsp;
coarser grained igneous rock called gabbro,&nbsp;
which has the same composition as the above&nbsp;&nbsp;
pillow basalt. Gabbro has larger crystals because&nbsp;
it cools much more slowly, as it is not near&nbsp;&nbsp;
the ocean water. In fact, the gabbro layer is&nbsp;
basically the crystallized remnants of the magma&nbsp;&nbsp;
chamber that was responsible for feeding all the&nbsp;
volcanism along the ridge. The upper portion of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the gabbro has a uniform structure, while the&nbsp;
bottom portion displays a layered or cumulate&nbsp;&nbsp;
structure, caused by dense crystals forming in&nbsp;
the magma chamber and sinking down to the bottom.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The basalt and gabbro of the upper oceanic&nbsp;
crust is composed mainly of calcium-rich&nbsp;&nbsp;
plagioclase feldspar, or CaAl2Si2O8, augite, or&nbsp;
CaMgSi2O6, and magnesium-rich olivine, or Mg2SiO4.&nbsp;
Finally, at the very bottom of the lithosphere&nbsp;
is rock called layered peridotite. The upper&nbsp;&nbsp;
portion of this layer is composed of crystallized&nbsp;
ultramafic partial melts of the mantle below,&nbsp;&nbsp;
called wehrlite. This material represents the&nbsp;
first and most undifferentiated magma created&nbsp;&nbsp;
at the ridge. As magma rises, it cools&nbsp;
and crystallizes out the most refractory&nbsp;&nbsp;
minerals first, which causes the magma&nbsp;
to change composition as it rises,&nbsp;&nbsp;
becoming more felsic, or rich in silica.&nbsp;
This process is called differentiation.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Wehrlite is rock that is composed mainly&nbsp;
of augite and olivine, and as it rises,&nbsp;&nbsp;
much of the olivine crystallizes out, leaving&nbsp;
behind a more silica-rich melt that eventually&nbsp;&nbsp;
forms the basaltic, feldspar-rich pillow lavas of&nbsp;
the ocean floor. Beneath the wehrlite is another&nbsp;&nbsp;
cumulate layer, or layer composed mainly of&nbsp;
olivine crystals that formed in the wehrlite&nbsp;&nbsp;
magma chamber and sank to the bottom. Finally,&nbsp;
at the very bottom is the unmelted residuum&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the original mantle which is composed mainly&nbsp;
of the olivine-rich rocks harzburgite and dunite.&nbsp;
The entire sequence of rocks that comprise oceanic&nbsp;
crust, from the layered peridotite on the bottom&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the sediment on top, is called an ophiolite.&nbsp;
Ophiolite outcrops can be found in Greece,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and most notably, Oman. The Semali ophiolite,&nbsp;
which was formed along a 95-million-year-old&nbsp;&nbsp;
mid-ocean ridge, is one of the largest&nbsp;
and best exposed ophiolites in the world.&nbsp;&nbsp;
As the ocean basin closed 80 million years ago,&nbsp;
the entire section of oceanic crust was thrust&nbsp;&nbsp;
onto the southeastern corner of the Arabian&nbsp;
Peninsula during the collision of the Arabian and&nbsp;&nbsp;
Eurasian plates. Ophiolites are typically the last&nbsp;
sliver of oceanic crust that evades subduction and&nbsp;&nbsp;
gets smushed between the two continents right&nbsp;
before they collide. Today, scientists use the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Semali ophiolite as a point of reference&nbsp;
for seismic measurements of oceanic crust.&nbsp;
So, with that we conclude a broad assessment of&nbsp;
all the layers of the earth. Now it’s time to zoom&nbsp;&nbsp;
in from this grander scale down to things that are&nbsp;
much more familiar and readily observable. What&nbsp;&nbsp;
are rocks themselves made of? Let’s spend the next&nbsp;
few tutorials answering this important question.
