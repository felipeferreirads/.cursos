In the previous tutorial we learned that&nbsp;
prior to the early 1900s, geologists used&nbsp;&nbsp;
relative dating to estimate the age of the&nbsp;
Earth. These estimates varied dramatically,&nbsp;&nbsp;
ranging from millions to billions of years.&nbsp;
The main reason for this uncertainty is that&nbsp;&nbsp;
there is no accurate way to determine how much&nbsp;
missing time unconformities represent, but once&nbsp;&nbsp;
radiometric dating was employed, the combination&nbsp;
of the two dating methods produced a very detailed&nbsp;&nbsp;
and accurate timeline of Earth’s history.
Radiometric dating, or absolute dating,&nbsp;&nbsp;
is a method of extracting exact ages from rocks&nbsp;
that utilizes the concept of nuclear decay, where&nbsp;&nbsp;
radioactive nuclides emit a high energy particle&nbsp;
to become a nuclide of some other element.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In order for radiometric dating to be applicable,&nbsp;
certain prerequisite criteria must be met. Rocks&nbsp;&nbsp;
must form and then become closed systems where&nbsp;
there is no exchange of atoms between the rock&nbsp;&nbsp;
and its environment. The best rocks with which&nbsp;
to use radiometric dating are igneous rocks that&nbsp;&nbsp;
cooled quicky and have not been reheated above the&nbsp;
blocking temperature, which is the temperature at&nbsp;&nbsp;
which parent and daughter isotopes can be lost&nbsp;
to the environment. Sedimentary rocks cannot be&nbsp;&nbsp;
dated using absolute dating, but their constituent&nbsp;
mineral grains can be. For example, in a previous&nbsp;&nbsp;
tutorial we mentioned the Jack Hills Conglomerate,&nbsp;
which was deposited about 3 billion years ago,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it contains the oldest terrestrial&nbsp;
material found on Earth, in the form of&nbsp;&nbsp;
4.4-billion-year-old zircon crystals. Metamorphic&nbsp;
rocks are difficult to date with radiometric&nbsp;&nbsp;
dating because the process of metamorphism can&nbsp;
involve hot fluids, which can add or remove parent&nbsp;&nbsp;
and daughter isotopes that are used to date the&nbsp;
rock. But for many rocks it is highly reliable,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so let’s learn more about how this works.
Radiometric dating was first documented in 1907&nbsp;&nbsp;
by Dr. Bertram Boltwood after he discovered that&nbsp;
uranium decays into lead, and that the uranium to&nbsp;&nbsp;
lead ratio present in a rock would vary based on&nbsp;
the rock’s age. We’ve discussed nuclear decay in&nbsp;&nbsp;
some detail over in the general chemistry&nbsp;
series, so check out this tutorial if you&nbsp;&nbsp;
need a thorough refresher on nuclear stability,&nbsp;
nuclear reactions, and applications. Otherwise,&nbsp;&nbsp;
let’s reiterate the main concepts and&nbsp;
contextualize them. The utility of radiometric&nbsp;&nbsp;
dating is based on the existence of isotopes,&nbsp;
or atoms of the same element with differing&nbsp;&nbsp;
numbers of neutrons. Carbon, for example, has&nbsp;
three naturally-occurring isotopes: carbon-12,&nbsp;&nbsp;
carbon-13, and carbon-14. Of these, only carbon-14&nbsp;
is radioactive, due to an unfavorable proton to&nbsp;&nbsp;
neutron ratio. It will therefore break down into&nbsp;
nitrogen-14 via beta decay, or the emission of&nbsp;&nbsp;
an electron which causes a neutron to become&nbsp;
a proton, thereby transmuting the nuclide. All&nbsp;&nbsp;
radioactive isotopes decay at a specific rate that&nbsp;
is represented by the isotope’s decay constant,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or number of disintegrations per year, which can&nbsp;
be used to calculate its half-life, or the amount&nbsp;&nbsp;
of time it takes for half of the radioactive&nbsp;
parent nuclide to decay into the daughter nuclide.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The consistency of this half-life is extremely&nbsp;
reliable, and does not depend on any aspect of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the environment. It will be the same everywhere&nbsp;
in the universe, and under any set of conditions.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And because the isotopes of a given element also&nbsp;
have a very reliable natural abundance, which we&nbsp;&nbsp;
use to determine atomic mass, it is a relatively&nbsp;
straightforward matter to compare some isotopic&nbsp;&nbsp;
ratio in a particular geologic material with the&nbsp;
naturally occurring ratio in matter which is being&nbsp;&nbsp;
formed and freely exchanged with its environment,&nbsp;
like the way that carbon-14 is produced by cosmic&nbsp;&nbsp;
rays from the sun and maintains a nearly constant&nbsp;
concentration in the atmosphere. This comparison&nbsp;&nbsp;
allows us to determine how many half-lives have&nbsp;
elapsed, and therefore the age of the object.&nbsp;
Each radioisotope has a unique time period over&nbsp;
which it is useful for dating, which is related&nbsp;&nbsp;
to its half-life. Namely, the parent must have&nbsp;
decayed enough to produce a measurable amount&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the daughter isotope, but not so much that the&nbsp;
parent has almost totally disintegrated. Here are&nbsp;&nbsp;
a few of the most commonly used parent-daughter&nbsp;
pairs and the ages over which they are useful.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14, 300 to 50,000 years.&nbsp;
Potassium-40 and argon-40, 100,000 to 4.3 billion&nbsp;&nbsp;
years. Uranium-238 and lead-206, 1 million to 4.5&nbsp;
billion years. Potassium-argon dating is used to&nbsp;&nbsp;
date rock with potassium-containing minerals, such&nbsp;
as biotite and potassium feldspar. Uranium-lead&nbsp;&nbsp;
dating is often used to date the oldest materials&nbsp;
on Earth, primarily because these old materials&nbsp;&nbsp;
are almost exclusively zircon grains, which take&nbsp;
up small amounts of uranium, but do not take up&nbsp;&nbsp;
lead, meaning that all the lead which can be found&nbsp;
in zircon accumulates by the decay of uranium.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This is an important assumption that must be made&nbsp;
in order to do some types of radiometric dating,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is that the material being analyzed did not&nbsp;
contain any isotopes of the daughter element at&nbsp;&nbsp;
the time it was crystallized. However,&nbsp;
isochron dating does not require this.&nbsp;
Let’s now go over how the radiometric dating&nbsp;
process works. First, a suitable rock and an&nbsp;&nbsp;
isotope system must be chosen. Let’s say&nbsp;
our hypothetical rock contains biotite as&nbsp;&nbsp;
its only source of potassium, so we will use the&nbsp;
potassium-argon system. Geologists must then check&nbsp;&nbsp;
the rock’s minerals with a microscope to ensure&nbsp;
that the rock has not been hydrothermally altered&nbsp;&nbsp;
in any way. The sample is then analyzed by a mass&nbsp;
spectrometer to determine the amount of radiogenic&nbsp;&nbsp;
argon, which can then be used to calculate the age&nbsp;
using the following formula, where 40Art is the&nbsp;&nbsp;
current amount of radiogenic argon, 40Art0 is the&nbsp;
amount of argon-40 at the time of crystallization,&nbsp;&nbsp;
40Kt0 is the amount of potassium-40 at the time&nbsp;
of crystallization, λ is the decay constant,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is 5.543 x 10-10, and λ_e/λ is the fraction&nbsp;
of potassium-40 that decays via electron capture,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is 0.1048, as potassium-40 can also&nbsp;
decay to calcium-40 through beta emission.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Next, because argon is not taken up&nbsp;
into biotite during crystallization,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we can assume that 40Art0 is zero. We can&nbsp;
also calculate 40Kt0 simply by knowing the&nbsp;&nbsp;
amount of potassium in biotite, which&nbsp;
is 4.49 x 10-4 moles potassium per gram&nbsp;&nbsp;
biotite and multiplying it by the fraction of&nbsp;
potassium 40 in nature, which is 1.19 x 10-4.&nbsp;&nbsp;
After rearranging the equation we arrive at this,&nbsp;
and we can now solve for t. A rock that contains&nbsp;&nbsp;
5 x 10-10 moles of argon per gram of biotite&nbsp;
would have a radiogenic age of 154 million years.&nbsp;
There are other isotope pairs, such as&nbsp;
rubidium-strontium and uranium-lead, where age is&nbsp;&nbsp;
determined by analyzing the relevant isotopes of&nbsp;
each mineral of a rock and graphing them. In the&nbsp;&nbsp;
case of rubidium-strontium, rubidium-87 breaks&nbsp;
down into strontium-87 through negatron decay,&nbsp;&nbsp;
another way of saying beta emission. Here,&nbsp;
rubidium-87 is the parent isotope, strontium-87&nbsp;&nbsp;
is the daughter isotope, and strontium-86 is the&nbsp;
non-radiogenic isotope of the daughter element.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Making a graph with the strontium-87 to 86 ratio&nbsp;
on the y-axis and the rubidium-87 to strontium-86&nbsp;&nbsp;
ratio on the x-axis, and plotting the values&nbsp;
for the various minerals contained in the rock,&nbsp;&nbsp;
creates a straight line called an isochron.&nbsp;
Steeper slopes indicate older samples where&nbsp;&nbsp;
more decay has occurred over a long period&nbsp;
of time. The exact age can be determined&nbsp;&nbsp;
by dividing the slope of the isochron&nbsp;
by the decay constant. Furthermore,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the y-intercept of the isochron gives the ratio of&nbsp;
strontium 87 to 86 at the time of crystallization.&nbsp;
So that covers some basic information regarding&nbsp;
relative and absolute dating methods, which we&nbsp;&nbsp;
can use to determine the age of geologic&nbsp;
features, and even the Earth itself. It&nbsp;&nbsp;
is quite astounding that we are able to probe&nbsp;
nature to such a sophisticated degree that we&nbsp;&nbsp;
are able to get answers to questions that have&nbsp;
profound philosophical impact, such as the age&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the world we live on, but this is simply a&nbsp;
testament to the power of scientific inquiry,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the efforts of those who dedicate themselves&nbsp;
to expanding the breadth of scientific knowledge.
