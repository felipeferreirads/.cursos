Throughout this series, we have discussed&nbsp;
many facets of mineralogy, such as structures,&nbsp;&nbsp;
classification, and formation. Now it’s time&nbsp;
to go into more depth regarding one process in&nbsp;&nbsp;
particular: weathering. All sedimentary&nbsp;
rocks are formed through weathering,&nbsp;&nbsp;
a combination of different forces and conditions&nbsp;
that break down rocks exposed at the surface into&nbsp;&nbsp;
smaller fragments. Geologists divide weathering&nbsp;
into two categories: physical and chemical.&nbsp;
Physical weathering is the collection of processes&nbsp;
that break rocks down mechanically. More simply,&nbsp;&nbsp;
one big rock becomes many small rocks. By&nbsp;
contrast, chemical weathering involves the&nbsp;&nbsp;
chemical alteration of minerals, including&nbsp;
acid-base reactions, redox reactions,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and dissolution. We will now discuss physical&nbsp;
weathering, which greatly accelerates chemical&nbsp;&nbsp;
weathering by breaking rocks into smaller pieces&nbsp;
and increasing the surface area available for&nbsp;&nbsp;
chemical reaction. There are many mechanisms of&nbsp;
physical weathering that operate in different&nbsp;&nbsp;
climates. For example, in temperate areas of&nbsp;
the world that experience freezing winters,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the freeze-thaw process is the dominant process.&nbsp;
Because ice has a 9% greater volume than liquid&nbsp;&nbsp;
water, when any preexisting void in a rock,&nbsp;
such as a fracture, fills with water which later&nbsp;&nbsp;
freezes, the expansion of the water applies&nbsp;
a force that is on the order of 1 kilobar,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or around 15,000 psi, which pushes outward in&nbsp;
all directions and creates new fractures. In&nbsp;&nbsp;
arid regions of the world, the growth&nbsp;
of secondary minerals in fractures,&nbsp;&nbsp;
such as gypsum or other salts, has a similar&nbsp;
effect to the freezing of water we just mentioned.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Tropical environments do not experience much&nbsp;
physical weathering; however, they have intense&nbsp;&nbsp;
chemical weathering due to abundant precipitation&nbsp;
and high temperatures. Physical weathering also&nbsp;&nbsp;
has a biologic component. For example, the&nbsp;
growth of tree roots into small rock openings&nbsp;&nbsp;
can widen and open them into larger fractures.&nbsp;
The branching root-like hyphae of fungi have&nbsp;&nbsp;
a similar effect. The burrowing of animals like&nbsp;
moles can bring fragments of rock to the surface,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where they are chemically weathered more rapidly.
Though not considered weathering, the formation&nbsp;&nbsp;
of regular sets of joints, or extensional&nbsp;
fractures, in bedrock greatly accelerate&nbsp;&nbsp;
the weathering process. All rocks contain&nbsp;
fractures, which form in aligned perpendicular&nbsp;&nbsp;
fashion to its least compressive stress. It&nbsp;
is common for a geologic formation to form&nbsp;&nbsp;
regularly spaced sets of joints as it is being&nbsp;
uplifted to the surface, and the stress field&nbsp;&nbsp;
changes from a state of horizontal compression to&nbsp;
horizontal tension as the lithostatic pressure,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or pressure from surrounding rocks, is reduced.&nbsp;
These joints tend to be perpendicular to the&nbsp;&nbsp;
surface and occur in sets of two at around 90&nbsp;
degrees, though they can be much more complex.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Next time you are driving along the highway,&nbsp;
notice how nearby rocks are broken along planar&nbsp;&nbsp;
surfaces, forming a step-like pattern. That is&nbsp;
not the work of humans, but of mother nature. At&nbsp;&nbsp;
any rate, joints provide starting areas for&nbsp;
the other mechanisms of physical weathering&nbsp;&nbsp;
to operate and since they’re often filled with&nbsp;
groundwater, they make great areas for thirsty&nbsp;&nbsp;
tree roots to enter in search of water.
With physical weathering covered,&nbsp;&nbsp;
let’s move forward and take a&nbsp;
look at chemical weathering next.
