We often think of Earth’s surface as a&nbsp;
well-explored place that is thoroughly colonized&nbsp;&nbsp;
by humans. To some, it may seem as though we know&nbsp;
all there is to know about this land we inhabit.&nbsp;&nbsp;
However, the truth is that most of the&nbsp;
surface of our planet is unexplored,&nbsp;&nbsp;
due to it being covered with kilometers of water.&nbsp;
Of course, we are talking about the ocean floor.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It sounds crazy, but we know more about the&nbsp;
lunar surface than we do about the ocean floor.&nbsp;&nbsp;
So, why is the ocean floor so difficult to&nbsp;
explore? The primary difficulty is withstanding&nbsp;&nbsp;
the high pressures that result from the&nbsp;
weight of up to 11 kilometers of water,&nbsp;&nbsp;
yielding pressures of more than 1000 atmospheres.&nbsp;
Though the deepest areas of the ocean, trenches,&nbsp;&nbsp;
only represents a small area of the ocean,&nbsp;
its average depth is still a substantial 3.5&nbsp;&nbsp;
kilometers. But, as we know from studying Earth’s&nbsp;
layers, we do not have to visit an area of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Earth to know something about it. Oceanographers&nbsp;
use methods like sonar, which reflects&nbsp;&nbsp;
sonic waves off the ocean floor, to determine its&nbsp;
topography and create detailed bathymetric maps.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Instruments called echo sounders emit a sound&nbsp;
pulse, and then measure the amount of time it&nbsp;&nbsp;
takes the pulse to reach the ocean floor,&nbsp;
bounce off of it, and return to the sounder.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Since the speed of the pulse can vary with ocean&nbsp;
water’s salinity, pressure, and temperature,&nbsp;&nbsp;
engineers developed multibeam echosounders that&nbsp;
send out hundreds of sound pulses and can measure&nbsp;&nbsp;
the angle of return from each pulse. With the high&nbsp;
angular resolution of the multibeam echosounder,&nbsp;&nbsp;
scientists can measure ocean floor features&nbsp;
from multiple sides and create a more realistic&nbsp;&nbsp;
picture of what these features look like.
So, what type of landforms are on the ocean floor?&nbsp;&nbsp;
For the most part, the very bottom of the&nbsp;
ocean floor, called the abyssal plain,&nbsp;&nbsp;
is flat and featureless. At the center of an ocean&nbsp;
basin lies a mid-ocean ridge, where new oceanic&nbsp;&nbsp;
crust is being created. On either side of&nbsp;
an ocean lies a continental margin, which is&nbsp;&nbsp;
simply the transition zone from land to sea. The&nbsp;
continental margin is composed of three areas,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the continental shelf, which is a gently sloping&nbsp;
surface that makes up all shorelines, the&nbsp;&nbsp;
continental slope, a steep drop-off that marks the&nbsp;
edge of the continental crust, and the continental&nbsp;&nbsp;
rise, which is an area where sediment from the&nbsp;
continental slope piles up during underwater&nbsp;&nbsp;
avalanches called turbidity currents. The abyssal&nbsp;
plane, which is probably what you imagine when you&nbsp;&nbsp;
think of the ocean floor, is located between&nbsp;
the mid-ocean ridge and continental margin.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The abyssal plane ranges in depth from 3 to 6&nbsp;
kilometers and covers 70% of the ocean floor.&nbsp;&nbsp;
As we mentioned, it is predominately flat&nbsp;
and featureless, with the occasional trench&nbsp;&nbsp;
near areas experiencing subduction, or volcanic&nbsp;
seamount above hotspots, breaking up the monotony.&nbsp;
Lying atop the oceanic crust is a thick layer of&nbsp;
sediment, blanketing the ocean floor and providing&nbsp;&nbsp;
a habitat for many types of burrowing organisms.&nbsp;
Sediment thickness varies based mainly on the&nbsp;&nbsp;
age of the crust and the distance from land, with&nbsp;
the thickest sediments occurring closest to land,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where both the sediment supply is greatest and&nbsp;
oceanic crust is oldest. The largest sediment&nbsp;&nbsp;
deposits in today’s oceans are in the Bay of&nbsp;
Bengal and Gulf of Mexico, where they approach&nbsp;&nbsp;
20 kilometers thick in some spots. For comparison,&nbsp;
atop the youngest crust near mid-ocean ridges,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sediment may only be millimeters thick. As&nbsp;
sediment is progressively buried in basins,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the weight of the overburden causes the&nbsp;
sediment to compact and minerals such as&nbsp;&nbsp;
quartz and calcite begin to precipitate from the&nbsp;
mineral-laden pore waters, cementing sediment&nbsp;&nbsp;
grains together to form a sedimentary&nbsp;
rock. This combined process of compaction&nbsp;&nbsp;
and cementation is called lithification.
To fully understand sedimentary rocks, we need&nbsp;&nbsp;
to understand sediments, where they come from,&nbsp;
how they are transported, and what happens to them&nbsp;&nbsp;
during transport. There are two types of sediment&nbsp;
found in the ocean: terrigenous and biogenic.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Terrigenous sediment is made up of minerals and&nbsp;
small rocks that eroded from continental crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or more simply, the land. Wind and water, though&nbsp;
mainly water, carry sediment from the mountains&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the oceans. The reason that the Bay of Bengal&nbsp;
has such a thick sediment layer is that several&nbsp;&nbsp;
large river systems are dumping millions of tons&nbsp;
of eroded sediment from the rising Himalayas.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In a similar manner, the Mississippi river carries&nbsp;
loads of sediment from the eroding Rockies and&nbsp;&nbsp;
Appalachians into the Gulf of Mexico. This is&nbsp;
terrigenous sediment. Most terrigenous sediment&nbsp;&nbsp;
is transported to the oceans during large storms&nbsp;
and floods when river flow is fastest. This is&nbsp;&nbsp;
the same reason why you probably shake your orange&nbsp;
juice before pouring a glass. Shaking it increases&nbsp;&nbsp;
its velocity, allowing the juice to lift the pulp&nbsp;
into suspension. Once the sediment reaches the&nbsp;&nbsp;
open ocean, flow velocity drops, and it begins&nbsp;
to settle out by mass. Sand settles out first,&nbsp;&nbsp;
followed by silt, then mud, with clay being the&nbsp;
last to settle out, sometimes remaining suspended&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the ocean for hundreds to thousands of years.&nbsp;
Since the largest grains settle out first, sand&nbsp;&nbsp;
and silt are only found near the shoreline,&nbsp;
with mud accumulating further offshore, and&nbsp;&nbsp;
only the smallest clay particles reaching the open&nbsp;
ocean, slowly accumulating on the abyssal plane.&nbsp;
Biogenic sediments, or “oozes”, are made&nbsp;
of materials created by living organisms.&nbsp;&nbsp;
There are two categories of biogenic&nbsp;
sediment that accumulate on the ocean floor:&nbsp;&nbsp;
calcareous and siliceous. Calcareous biogenic&nbsp;
sediments are comprised of the calcium carbonate&nbsp;&nbsp;
skeletons from single-celled marine organisms like&nbsp;
foraminifera. However, since calcium carbonate&nbsp;&nbsp;
dissolves readily in colder, deeper water,&nbsp;
calcareous oozes are primarily found in warm,&nbsp;&nbsp;
shallow seas. The depth at which calcium&nbsp;
carbonate starts to dissolve in ocean water&nbsp;&nbsp;
is called the carbonate compensation depth,&nbsp;
abbreviated as CCD. Since the CCD is a function&nbsp;&nbsp;
of temperature and seawater chemistry, its exact&nbsp;
depth varies, being deeper in equatorial regions&nbsp;&nbsp;
and shallower near the poles. By mapping this&nbsp;
depth across the ocean, scientists created the&nbsp;&nbsp;
lysocline, which shows how the CCD changes with&nbsp;
location. Siliceous biogenic sediments are made&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the silica skeletons from single-celled marine&nbsp;
organisms like diatoms and radiolarians. Siliceous&nbsp;&nbsp;
oozes are essentially the opposite of calcareous&nbsp;
oozes in that the silica tends to dissolve&nbsp;&nbsp;
rapidly in the shallow ocean, whereas carbonate&nbsp;
rapidly dissolves in deep water, as we mentioned.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Given the high solubility in seawater,&nbsp;
siliceous oozes only form in areas where&nbsp;&nbsp;
diatoms and radiolarians are thriving,&nbsp;
and sedimentation can exceed dissolution.&nbsp;
Unlike the calcareous, siliceous oozes are likely&nbsp;
to be found in the cold, deep water at the poles&nbsp;&nbsp;
or in areas where upwelling occurs. Upwelling&nbsp;
is a process where strong winds and the Earth’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
rotation cause shallow waters to diverge, allowing&nbsp;
deep, cold water to rise to the surface, filling&nbsp;&nbsp;
the space created by the diverging surface waters.&nbsp;
Upwelling tends to occur along western shorelines&nbsp;&nbsp;
where winds blow from the North, which&nbsp;
when modified by the Coriolis Force,&nbsp;&nbsp;
cause an ocean current that moves away from&nbsp;
land, or 90 degrees to the right of the wind.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This movement of water at right angles to the&nbsp;
prevailing winds is called Eckman Transport.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Coriolis Force deflects moving objects&nbsp;
to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. This&nbsp;
applies to all moving fluids on Earth’s surface,&nbsp;&nbsp;
including the atmosphere.
With these different types&nbsp;&nbsp;
of sediments understood, let’s move forward and&nbsp;
get a closer look at the oceanic crust itself.
