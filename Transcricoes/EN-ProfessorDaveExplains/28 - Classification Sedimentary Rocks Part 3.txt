Now that we have covered both siliciclastic&nbsp;
and biogenic sedimentary rocks, we are ready&nbsp;&nbsp;
to move on to chemogenic rocks. These are rocks&nbsp;
that chemically precipitate from natural waters,&nbsp;&nbsp;
often involving evaporational concentration.&nbsp;
Hence, they are often called evaporite minerals.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Though there are other types of chemical&nbsp;
sedimentary rocks, such as iron formations&nbsp;&nbsp;
and phosphorite deposits, this tutorial will&nbsp;
focus on evaporite rocks since they are much more&nbsp;&nbsp;
common. Because evaporite rocks precipitate from&nbsp;
natural waters, they are dominated by minerals&nbsp;&nbsp;
that are salts. The carbonate, chloride,&nbsp;
and sulfate salts are especially common.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Evaporite rocks can form from the evaporation of&nbsp;
either freshwater or seawater, with the water’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
chemistry being the main factor determining which&nbsp;
evaporite minerals precipitate and in what order.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Marine evaporites are dominated by the calcium&nbsp;
sulfate minerals gypsum and anhydrite, as well as&nbsp;&nbsp;
halite, with lesser amounts of calcite, potassium&nbsp;
salts, and magnesium sulfate. Because seawater is&nbsp;&nbsp;
essentially globally homogeneous, the minerals&nbsp;
that are precipitated during its evaporation&nbsp;&nbsp;
follow a specific sequence. Calcite forms first,&nbsp;
followed by gypsum, then halite, concluding with&nbsp;&nbsp;
various potassium and magnesium-rich salts. This&nbsp;
sequence is dictated by the relative solubilities&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the minerals, with the most insoluble salts&nbsp;
precipitating first. If one were to entirely&nbsp;&nbsp;
evaporate a beaker of seawater, almost 80% of&nbsp;
the total precipitated salts would be halite.&nbsp;&nbsp;
On the other hand, nonmarine waters can&nbsp;
be much more compositionally variable,&nbsp;&nbsp;
reflecting local variations in bedrock mineralogy.&nbsp;
Though in general, nonmarine waters tend to be&nbsp;&nbsp;
enriched in bicarbonate and depleted in chlorine&nbsp;
with respect to seawater. The thickest and most&nbsp;&nbsp;
laterally extensive evaporite deposits were&nbsp;
formed from the evaporation of seawater and&nbsp;&nbsp;
are often predominantly composed of halite&nbsp;
and gypsum/anhydrite. Gypsum is the hydrated&nbsp;&nbsp;
version of calcium sulfate and readily converts to&nbsp;
anhydrite after burial. The conversion of gypsum&nbsp;&nbsp;
to anhydrite involves a 38% decrease in volume as&nbsp;
the lattice is dewatered, which often results in&nbsp;&nbsp;
the deformation of gypsum deposits, especially&nbsp;
since anhydrite is sometimes converted back to&nbsp;&nbsp;
gypsum later during uplift and exhumation. As&nbsp;
a result, calcium sulfate deposits are commonly&nbsp;&nbsp;
heavily distorted, even in tectonically quiescent&nbsp;
areas. Gypsum and anhydrite can form a nodular&nbsp;&nbsp;
texture, sometimes called chickenwire anhydrite.&nbsp;
It forms during the early stages of diagenesis&nbsp;&nbsp;
when gypsum crystals grow within deposits of&nbsp;
carbonate mud, displacing and compacting the mud.&nbsp;
Evaporite rocks are sometimes laminated, as in&nbsp;
the Castile Formation of New Mexico. It consists&nbsp;&nbsp;
of up to 300 feet of interbedded calcite and&nbsp;
gypsum with many of the individual layers&nbsp;&nbsp;
spanning great distances; in other words, it has&nbsp;
great lateral continuity. The cyclical variation&nbsp;&nbsp;
between calcite and gypsum deposition probably&nbsp;
represents some regular variation in climate,&nbsp;&nbsp;
perhaps being driven by alternating wet and dry&nbsp;
seasons. Recall that when seawater is evaporated,&nbsp;&nbsp;
calcite precipitates first, followed by gypsum,&nbsp;
then halite. Consider the state of the system&nbsp;&nbsp;
right as calcite precipitation stops and gypsum&nbsp;
precipitation begins. Why does calcite stop&nbsp;&nbsp;
forming? Let’s consider which variables we are&nbsp;
changing. We are concentrating the solution via&nbsp;&nbsp;
evaporation, so we are reducing the activity of&nbsp;
water. This would not stop calcite from forming.&nbsp;&nbsp;
We are also crystallizing calcite, and by doing&nbsp;
so, removing calcium carbonate from solution.&nbsp;&nbsp;
If we were to run out of either calcium or&nbsp;
carbonate ions, calcite would not be able to form.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Since gypsum is the next phase to precipitate,&nbsp;
we know that calcium is abundant, so we must be&nbsp;&nbsp;
running out of carbonate ions. If the chemistry&nbsp;
of seawater was different, and instead we ran out&nbsp;&nbsp;
of calcium before carbonate, the next mineral to&nbsp;
precipitate in sequence might have been nahcolite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or sodium bicarbonate, since there would be no&nbsp;
calcium left to make gypsum. So to summarize,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the crystallization sequence of natural solutions&nbsp;
is influenced by several chemical divides that&nbsp;&nbsp;
arise during its compositional evolution,&nbsp;
which is driven by the sequestration of&nbsp;&nbsp;
chemical components via crystallization. This&nbsp;
process is very similar to the differentiation&nbsp;&nbsp;
of magmas discussed in previous tutorials.
Let’s now apply this to the Castile Formation&nbsp;&nbsp;
to explain the cyclic repetition of calcite and&nbsp;
gypsum. If we start with calcite, then gypsum&nbsp;&nbsp;
would start to form once all the carbonate&nbsp;
is exhausted. To start forming calcite again,&nbsp;&nbsp;
carbonate would have to be added to the system,&nbsp;
which can easily be accomplished by inflow into&nbsp;&nbsp;
the basin via rivers and streams. Furthermore,&nbsp;
most rivers tend to have much higher amounts of&nbsp;&nbsp;
carbonate than sulfate. So, each calcite-gypsum&nbsp;
bundle probably represents an influx of fresh,&nbsp;&nbsp;
carbonate-rich water into the basin followed by&nbsp;
evaporation, leading to the depletion of carbonate&nbsp;&nbsp;
via calcite crystallization and the subsequent&nbsp;
precipitation of gypsum. This is interpreted to&nbsp;&nbsp;
represent cyclical variations in climate, with&nbsp;
calcite being deposited during wet periods,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and gypsum during dry periods. Above the Castile&nbsp;
formation is the Salado formation, which consists&nbsp;&nbsp;
of interbedded halite and gypsum, indicating that&nbsp;
the basin became more concentrated with time.&nbsp;
Halite deposits are often called “rock salt”&nbsp;
and can occur in deposits up to 2 kilometers&nbsp;&nbsp;
thick in some areas. Most large halite deposits&nbsp;
are thought to have formed from the evaporation&nbsp;&nbsp;
of seawater in large, restricted basins existing&nbsp;
in arid environments. Since there are no modern&nbsp;&nbsp;
analogs for the evaporation of seawater on such&nbsp;
a large scale, geologists have proposed several&nbsp;&nbsp;
models for the formation of such deposits. They&nbsp;
all involve large coastal depressions that are&nbsp;&nbsp;
separated from the ocean by a barrier of some&nbsp;
sort, such as a barrier bar, which is just a&nbsp;&nbsp;
subaerial ridge of beach sand. The barrier, or&nbsp;
sill, impedes mixing between the basin water and&nbsp;&nbsp;
ocean water, allowing the basin water to become&nbsp;
highly concentrated via evaporation. In order to&nbsp;&nbsp;
form kilometer-thick salt deposits, there must&nbsp;
be a regular influx of solutes into the basin,&nbsp;&nbsp;
providing the materials to make the salt. For&nbsp;
example, even if the entire Atlantic Ocean were&nbsp;&nbsp;
to be fully evaporated, the resultant salt deposit&nbsp;
would only be around 50 meters thick. This can be&nbsp;&nbsp;
accomplished in a few ways, such as seawater&nbsp;
washing in during storm surges, or the seepage&nbsp;&nbsp;
of seawater into the basin through the sill.
Evaporite rocks are extremely susceptible to&nbsp;&nbsp;
diagenesis because they are so soluble. Therefore,&nbsp;
it is very common to find substantial dissolution&nbsp;&nbsp;
and recrystallization, especially in older&nbsp;
deposits. Salt deposits also have the unusual&nbsp;&nbsp;
property of high mobility, causing them to behave&nbsp;
more like viscous magma than solid rock, if they&nbsp;&nbsp;
are buried deep enough. There are many places on&nbsp;
Earth where salt is rising up through the crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and even erupting from the surface in some cases,&nbsp;
as in the many salt glaciers of Iran. The movement&nbsp;&nbsp;
of salt after deposition, or “salt tectonics”,&nbsp;
creates many interesting geologic structures,&nbsp;&nbsp;
such as salt domes, which often make great&nbsp;
structural traps for oil and gas. The oil deposits&nbsp;&nbsp;
in the Gulf of Mexico are a great example of this.
With chemogenic rocks covered, we have completed&nbsp;&nbsp;
our assessment of the classification of&nbsp;
sedimentary rocks, and our analysis of&nbsp;&nbsp;
sedimentary rocks in general. Let’s move on to our&nbsp;
third and final type of rock, metamorphic rock.
