So far in this geology series we’ve focused on&nbsp;
large-scale geological structures and phenomena.&nbsp;&nbsp;
But now it’s time to zoom in and discuss what most&nbsp;
people think of first when geology comes to mind:&nbsp;&nbsp;
rocks and minerals. In the broadest sense, all&nbsp;
rocks are assemblages of specific elements and&nbsp;&nbsp;
compounds called minerals, but the precise&nbsp;
definition of a mineral depends on who you&nbsp;&nbsp;
ask. Nutritionists might use the word mineral&nbsp;
to describe elements like calcium and zinc,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are found in certain types of foods or&nbsp;
supplements. Biologists might define a mineral&nbsp;&nbsp;
as any inorganic material present within a living&nbsp;
organism. But how do geologists define minerals?&nbsp;
James D. Dana, an American geologist who created&nbsp;
the first, standardized classification system&nbsp;&nbsp;
for over 5,000 minerals in the 1850s, defined&nbsp;
a mineral as “... a naturally occurring solid&nbsp;&nbsp;
chemical substance formed through biogeochemical&nbsp;
processes, having characteristic chemical&nbsp;&nbsp;
composition, highly ordered atomic structure, and&nbsp;
specific physical properties.” The International&nbsp;&nbsp;
Mineralogical Association, or IMA, has built&nbsp;
off of Dana’s original definition and developed&nbsp;&nbsp;
a stringent list of criteria for mineral&nbsp;
status. Minerals must be naturally occurring,&nbsp;&nbsp;
crystalline solids that are formed by geological&nbsp;
processes, composed of elements or compounds,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and can be characterized by a standard chemical&nbsp;
formula. Amazingly, new minerals are discovered&nbsp;&nbsp;
and proposed to the IMA every month! For example,&nbsp;
just in May 2021, scientists discovered three new&nbsp;&nbsp;
arsenic and copper-containing minerals forming&nbsp;
along fissures in the Tolbachik volcano in Russia.&nbsp;
The most fundamental property of&nbsp;
a mineral is its crystallinity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or the arrangement of the mineral’s atoms&nbsp;
in a repeating three-dimensional framework&nbsp;&nbsp;
called a lattice, a term we should be familiar&nbsp;
with from the general chemistry series.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Some of the items you might find at a local&nbsp;
rock or gem shop, like opal or obsidian,&nbsp;&nbsp;
aren’t actually minerals at all. They fail to&nbsp;
qualify because their atomic structures are&nbsp;&nbsp;
amorphous, meaning that they lack a&nbsp;
repeating, three-dimensional crystal lattice.&nbsp;
Another defining characteristic of a mineral is&nbsp;
its elemental composition, which is represented&nbsp;&nbsp;
by a chemical formula that represents one formula&nbsp;
unit of the mineral’s structure. Mineralogists use&nbsp;&nbsp;
a mineral’s chemical formula along with its&nbsp;
crystalline structure to assign it a mineral&nbsp;&nbsp;
class. Classes are a convenient way to group&nbsp;
minerals together based on shared properties,&nbsp;&nbsp;
making it easier for geologists to identify&nbsp;
minerals and study rocks, especially in the field.&nbsp;
The IMA uses the Nickel-Strunz classification&nbsp;
system, heavily based on Dana’s original&nbsp;&nbsp;
classification scheme, which divides&nbsp;
all minerals into specific classes&nbsp;&nbsp;
based on their elemental composition, crystalline&nbsp;
structure, and bonding type. Bonding types are of&nbsp;&nbsp;
particular interest in mineralogy because the&nbsp;
nature of a mineral’s chemical bonds dictates&nbsp;&nbsp;
many of its physical properties. Covalent&nbsp;
bonds are the strongest type of chemical bond.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Minerals with predominately covalent bonds are&nbsp;
the hardest and most refractory minerals. Diamond,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is composed of linked carbon tetrahedra, is&nbsp;
the prime example of a covalently bonded mineral.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It is the hardest substance known and has a&nbsp;
melting point of over 4000 degrees Celsius.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For a comparison, most rocks begin to melt&nbsp;
somewhere between 700 and 1300 degrees Celsius.&nbsp;
Ionic bonds are the second strongest type of&nbsp;
chemical bond. Minerals with predominately&nbsp;&nbsp;
ionic bonds tend to be easier to break, and they&nbsp;
often fracture smoothly along planar surfaces.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Geologists call this quality “cleavage”, and&nbsp;
a mineral is said to have “good cleavage” if&nbsp;&nbsp;
it breaks along a single plane of atoms.&nbsp;
Minerals with ionic bonds also tend to be&nbsp;&nbsp;
highly symmetrical, have lower melting points,&nbsp;
and are highly soluble in water. Table salt,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or sodium chloride, and calcium sulfate are two&nbsp;
examples of minerals dominated by ionic bonds.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In the world of geology, sodium chloride is&nbsp;
called halite and calcium sulfate is anhydrite.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Halite exhibits a type of cleavage called&nbsp;
cubic cleavage, where the mineral breaks&nbsp;&nbsp;
along three orthogonal planes, creating little&nbsp;
symmetrical cubes when smashed with a hammer.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Calcite, or calcium carbonate exhibits&nbsp;
rhombohedral cleavage, where the mineral&nbsp;&nbsp;
breaks along planes at 60- and 120-degree angles,&nbsp;
creating miniature rhombohedrons when fractured.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Characterization of cleavage is an excellent&nbsp;
way to identify minerals in hand samples,&nbsp;&nbsp;
especially in the field. Most of Earth’s&nbsp;
ionically bonded minerals are evaporite minerals,&nbsp;&nbsp;
meaning they precipitate from an evaporating&nbsp;
body of water, such as a lake or sea.&nbsp;
Metallic bonds are the weakest type of chemical&nbsp;
bond, making their minerals soft and weak. Due to&nbsp;&nbsp;
the unique chemical properties of metallic bonds,&nbsp;
valence electrons can flow freely throughout the&nbsp;&nbsp;
entire lattice, which gives rise to the high&nbsp;
conductivity of electricity and heat exhibited&nbsp;&nbsp;
by metallic minerals. Metallic minerals can be&nbsp;
made of a single element, like iron or platinum,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or can be composed of an alloy, or mixture of&nbsp;
metals. All metals have a crystalline structure,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with most being cubic. Metals are shiny,&nbsp;
opaque, and very insoluble in water.&nbsp;
Now that we have discussed the three main bonding&nbsp;
types, it is important to note that chemical bonds&nbsp;&nbsp;
occur along a spectrum, with metallic, covalent,&nbsp;
and ionic being endmembers, or distinctive types&nbsp;&nbsp;
of bonding. For example, there is a complete&nbsp;
spectrum of bonding ranging from pure metallic&nbsp;&nbsp;
to pure covalent, as well as from pure covalent to&nbsp;
pure ionic. It is not possible to have a bond that&nbsp;&nbsp;
is mixed ionic and metallic in nature, as they are&nbsp;
not compatible. Ionic bonds involve the creation&nbsp;&nbsp;
of cations and anions by an exchange of electrons&nbsp;
between atoms of contrasting electronegativity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
whereas the bonding between metals involves free&nbsp;
roaming valence electrons travelling between&nbsp;&nbsp;
atoms of near identical electronegativity;&nbsp;
they are essentially mutually exclusive.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Most of Earth’s minerals are composed of&nbsp;
metals bonded to silicate anions and the&nbsp;&nbsp;
nature of these bonds is mixed ionic-covalent.
So, we now understand what a mineral is, and know&nbsp;&nbsp;
some details regarding their basic properties,&nbsp;
such as crystallinity, and the types of chemical&nbsp;&nbsp;
bonding that are present in their lattice&nbsp;
structure. In the next tutorial we will continue&nbsp;&nbsp;
to discuss how bonding and crystal structure&nbsp;
affects the physical properties of minerals.
