In the previous tutorial, we learned about&nbsp;
how metamorphic rocks form. But there are&nbsp;&nbsp;
differences in the metamorphic process that&nbsp;
result in different types of metamorphic rock.&nbsp;&nbsp;
More specifically, geologists group metamorphic&nbsp;
rocks into “facies”, which represent a common&nbsp;&nbsp;
group of minerals that form over certain ranges&nbsp;
of pressure-temperature conditions. This makes&nbsp;&nbsp;
it much easier to compare metamorphic rocks&nbsp;
from different locations around the world,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and also to relate metamorphic rocks&nbsp;
to certain geologic environments.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For example, the sodium amphibole glaucophane&nbsp;
is only formed at low temperatures and very high&nbsp;&nbsp;
pressures, and since these conditions are almost&nbsp;
exclusively found in subduction zones, you can&nbsp;&nbsp;
reasonably assert that any glaucophane-containing&nbsp;
rock formed there. This is how geologists use&nbsp;&nbsp;
metamorphic facies in a nutshell. Rock X&nbsp;
contains minerals A, B and C, therefore&nbsp;&nbsp;
it must have formed in geologic environment Z.&nbsp;
There are eleven different metamorphic facies,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which can be put into three groups that&nbsp;
describe the major types of metamorphism;&nbsp;&nbsp;
they are the low P/T, medium P/T, and high P/T&nbsp;
series, where P is pressure and T is temperature.&nbsp;
The low P/T series describes contact metamorphism,&nbsp;
which occurs when an igneous body intrudes into&nbsp;&nbsp;
an area through numerous fractures and&nbsp;
“bakes” the adjacent rock, so to speak.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Contact metamorphism occurs at low pressure, since&nbsp;
igneous intrusions mainly occur at shallow depths&nbsp;&nbsp;
and at high temperatures, due to the heat supplied&nbsp;
by the magma. A similar type of metamorphism,&nbsp;&nbsp;
hydrothermal, occurs when hot aqueous&nbsp;
fluids move though large areas of rock,&nbsp;&nbsp;
supplying heat and chemically active ions&nbsp;
that cause recrystallization. This occurs&nbsp;&nbsp;
on a large scale around mid-ocean ridges where&nbsp;
heated ocean water circulates through the crust,&nbsp;&nbsp;
altering olivine and pyroxene to serpentine&nbsp;
(Mg2SiO4 + MgSiO3 + 2H2O = Mg3Si2O5(OH)4). This&nbsp;&nbsp;
is the water that is later released during&nbsp;
subduction and is responsible for volcanism there.&nbsp;
Generally, low P/T metamorphism occurs&nbsp;
below a pressure of about 2 kilobars. We&nbsp;&nbsp;
will now consider the progressive contact&nbsp;
metamorphism of a mafic rock, like basalt.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It should be noted that at lower temperatures,&nbsp;
less than 300 degrees Celsius, recrystallization&nbsp;&nbsp;
may not occur due to poor reaction kinetics. The&nbsp;
lowest grade metamorphic facies is the zeolite&nbsp;&nbsp;
facies, occurring below 200 degrees Celsius.&nbsp;
This is our starting point. Zeolite refers to a&nbsp;&nbsp;
group of framework silicate minerals that have&nbsp;
large amounts of open space in their lattice.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Cat litter often contains zeolites, due to their&nbsp;
ability to absorb large amounts of water. Once we&nbsp;&nbsp;
pass about 300 degrees Celsius, we encounter&nbsp;
the albite-epidote hornfels facies. Hornfels&nbsp;&nbsp;
is a general name for a rock that has undergone&nbsp;
contact metamorphism, and albite-epidote refers to&nbsp;&nbsp;
the stable minerals under these conditions. Albite&nbsp;
is the sodium endmember of plagioclase feldspar,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and epidote is a somewhat obscure iron-containing&nbsp;
sorosilicate. At around 500 degrees Celsius,&nbsp;&nbsp;
hornblende will begin to crystallize as we&nbsp;
move into the hornblende hornfels facies.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Hornblende is a calcium amphibole that appears&nbsp;
over a wide range of metamorphic conditions.&nbsp;&nbsp;
At around 650 degrees Celsius, it becomes too&nbsp;
hot for the water-containing amphiboles to exist,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and they break down into pyroxene plus quartz&nbsp;
plus water; these conditions mark the pyroxene&nbsp;&nbsp;
hornfels facies. Finally, at a temperature of 800&nbsp;
degrees Celsius and a pressure of only 2 kilobars,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we reach the sanidinite facies, where the&nbsp;
potassium feldspar polymorph, sanidine,&nbsp;&nbsp;
forms. However, these conditions are&nbsp;
rare, since it is difficult to attain&nbsp;&nbsp;
such high temperatures so shallow in the crust.
The medium P/T series encompasses orogenic, or&nbsp;&nbsp;
mountain-building events, where the intense heat&nbsp;
and pressure associated with colliding continents&nbsp;&nbsp;
metamorphoses the rock near the collision.&nbsp;
Medium P/T metamorphism generally occurs at&nbsp;&nbsp;
pressures between about 2 and 12 kilobars. Let’s&nbsp;
now consider the progressive orogenic metamorphism&nbsp;&nbsp;
of a basalt, and like before, we start in the&nbsp;
zeolite facies. From there, we move into the&nbsp;&nbsp;
prehnite-pumpellyite facies starting at around&nbsp;
200 degrees Celsius and 3 kilobars. The next&nbsp;&nbsp;
three facies probably represent the overwhelming&nbsp;
majority of all metamorphic rocks on Earth as they&nbsp;&nbsp;
span a large range of P-T conditions. Between 300&nbsp;
and 500 degrees Celsius is the greenschist facies,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which gets its name from the green color of the&nbsp;
chlorite minerals that crystallize under these&nbsp;&nbsp;
conditions. Other common minerals in greenschist&nbsp;
rocks are the magnesium-rich amphibole actinolite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
epidote, and calcite. Between 500 and 700&nbsp;
degrees Celsius is the amphibolite facies,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is unsurprisingly marked by the formation&nbsp;
of amphiboles, especially hornblende along&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the high-pressure mineral garnet. Common&nbsp;
coexisting minerals are plagioclase and augite.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Finally, we reach the granulite facies, which&nbsp;
occur from 700 degrees Celsius all the way up&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the onset of melting. It is characterized&nbsp;
by a largely anhydrous mineral assemblage&nbsp;&nbsp;
that commonly includes clinopyroxene,&nbsp;
orthopyroxene, plagioclase, and garnet.&nbsp;
The high P/T series mainly occurs in subduction&nbsp;
zones, where cold oceanic lithosphere is brought&nbsp;&nbsp;
to great depths, where the temperature would&nbsp;
normally be much higher. In these environments,&nbsp;&nbsp;
P/T conditions can range from 4 kilobars and 200&nbsp;
degrees Celsius all the way up to 50 kilobars and&nbsp;&nbsp;
950 degrees Celsius, which is equivalent to a&nbsp;
depth of 170 kilometers, meaning that pieces of&nbsp;&nbsp;
oceanic lithosphere are capable of being subducted&nbsp;
to this incredible depth and later returned to the&nbsp;&nbsp;
surface without melting. Let’s now consider&nbsp;
the progressive high-pressure metamorphism of&nbsp;&nbsp;
basalt. Like with orogenic metamorphism, we begin&nbsp;
with the zeolite facies and then move through the&nbsp;&nbsp;
prehnite-pumpellyite facies. From here, we move&nbsp;
into the blueschist facies at around 250 degrees&nbsp;&nbsp;
Celsius and 5 kilobars. The blueschist facies&nbsp;
gets its name from the blue color of the amphibole&nbsp;&nbsp;
glaucophane, which is abundant in these rocks.&nbsp;
Other minerals in blueschists are garnet, epidote,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and aragonite, which is the high-pressure&nbsp;
polymorph of calcite. As the pressure and&nbsp;&nbsp;
temperature are increased to around 14 kilobars&nbsp;
and 500 degrees Celsius, we move into the eclogite&nbsp;&nbsp;
facies, which is dominated by omphacite, garnet,&nbsp;
and kyanite, and which exists with glaucophane&nbsp;&nbsp;
at lower pressures. Omphacite is a pyroxene&nbsp;
solid solution between augite and jadeite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and kyanite is the high-pressure polymorph of&nbsp;
Al2SiO5. By the time we reach about 30 kilobars&nbsp;&nbsp;
and 600 degrees Celsius, amphibole completely&nbsp;
breaks down and quartz transforms into coesite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
its high-pressure polymorph, and at around&nbsp;
35 kilobars, graphite turns to diamond.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Metamorphism that produces coesite and diamond&nbsp;
is said to be ultra-high-pressure metamorphism.&nbsp;
In addition to examining the progressive&nbsp;
metamorphism of mafic rocks, we have also&nbsp;&nbsp;
looked at metamorphic intensity using a mudstone&nbsp;
as an example. Let’s talk a bit more about the&nbsp;&nbsp;
metamorphism of felsic rocks. First off, the&nbsp;
parent rock, or rock that is to be metamorphosed,&nbsp;&nbsp;
is called the protolith. So let’s consider&nbsp;
some different protoliths. Let’s start simple,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with sandstone as our protolith. Since&nbsp;
sandstones tend to be very rich in quartz,&nbsp;&nbsp;
when they are metamorphosed the quartz just&nbsp;
recrystallizes into larger crystals. This type&nbsp;&nbsp;
of rock is called quartzite. The metamorphism of&nbsp;
felsic igneous rocks, like granite, is also pretty&nbsp;&nbsp;
straightforward and most of what will happen&nbsp;
is recrystallization of the original minerals&nbsp;&nbsp;
into larger crystals. High grade metamorphism&nbsp;
of a granite will transform it into a gneiss.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The metamorphism of limestone, which typically&nbsp;
contain mainly calcite with some quartz, involves&nbsp;&nbsp;
a lot of calcite recrystallization forming the&nbsp;
metamorphic rock, marble. Other minerals like&nbsp;&nbsp;
quartz will react with calcite to form silicate&nbsp;
minerals, such as wollastonite, or CaSiO3.&nbsp;
One of the most common types of rock that&nbsp;
get metamorphosed in mountain building events&nbsp;&nbsp;
are mudstones, as these are the most abundant&nbsp;
sedimentary rock. They also show the most dramatic&nbsp;&nbsp;
transformations as they are metamorphosed,&nbsp;
going form slate to phyllite, to schist,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and finally gneiss, as previously discussed.&nbsp;
We can relate these types of metamorphic rocks&nbsp;&nbsp;
to our metamorphic facies and the following are&nbsp;
equivalent: slate to prehnite-pumpellyite facies,&nbsp;&nbsp;
phyllite to greenschist facies, schist&nbsp;
to amphibolite facies, and gneiss to&nbsp;&nbsp;
granulite facies. Being rich in aluminum and&nbsp;
potassium, the minerals that recrystallize&nbsp;&nbsp;
during metamorphism will also be rich in these&nbsp;
elements. In addition to quartz and feldspar,&nbsp;&nbsp;
aluminum-rich minerals like the aluminosilicate&nbsp;
polymorphs, staurolite, and cordierite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and potassium-rich sheet silicates like muscovite&nbsp;
and biotite commonly occur in these rocks.&nbsp;
And that concludes our introduction to metamorphic&nbsp;&nbsp;
rocks, and therefore to the&nbsp;
types of rocks in general.
