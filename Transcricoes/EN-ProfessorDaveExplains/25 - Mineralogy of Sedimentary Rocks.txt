Now that we know something about the general types&nbsp;
of sedimentary rocks, lets further discuss their&nbsp;&nbsp;
mineralogy, starting with terrigenous, or clastic&nbsp;
rocks. Since these rocks are made of pieces&nbsp;&nbsp;
of preexisting rock that have been weathered,&nbsp;
eroded, and transported, we must consider how the&nbsp;&nbsp;
common terrigenous minerals change during these&nbsp;
processes. Any rock or sediment that is exposed&nbsp;&nbsp;
at the surface will be under constant attack from&nbsp;
acidic precipitation and groundwater; therefore,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the minerals that survive and make it to the&nbsp;
depositional area must be relatively unreactive.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The reactivity of the silicate minerals at the&nbsp;
surface tends to follow Bowen’s Reaction Series,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the mafic minerals at the top being highly&nbsp;
reactive, and the felsic minerals at the bottom&nbsp;&nbsp;
being least reactive, the most stable of them&nbsp;
being quartz and the clay minerals. Incidentally,&nbsp;&nbsp;
these are the minerals most commonly found&nbsp;
in clastic sedimentary rocks. A sediment’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
maturity refers to the amount of time that it&nbsp;
spent exposed at the surface and can be gauged&nbsp;&nbsp;
based on the relative amounts of quartz plus clay&nbsp;
to feldspar, which breaks down to clay over time.&nbsp;&nbsp;
So the longer a pile of sediment sits near the&nbsp;
surface, the more feldspar that becomes clay.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Now think about which minerals you would least&nbsp;
expect to find in a terrigenous sedimentary rock.&nbsp;&nbsp;
What sort of chemical properties might they have?&nbsp;
Not surprisingly, the most soluble minerals, the&nbsp;&nbsp;
salts, are rarely ever found as clastic sediments;&nbsp;
this includes all the evaporite minerals.&nbsp;
In addition to being chemically altered, or as&nbsp;
geologists say, chemically weathered, during&nbsp;&nbsp;
transport, grains of sediment are also abraded,&nbsp;
as they crash into each other during turbulent&nbsp;&nbsp;
flow. This abrasion makes the grains more rounded,&nbsp;
and the longer a grain of sediment is transported,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the more rounded it becomes. In addition, other&nbsp;
processes that operate during transport act to&nbsp;&nbsp;
sort the grains based on their diameter. Consider&nbsp;
a sandy beach; the constant breaking of the waves&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the shoreline continuously rinses out the&nbsp;
silt and mud particles since they are small&nbsp;&nbsp;
enough to be suspended in the ocean water, which&nbsp;
get carried offshore until the water is calmer.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The silt will drop out first, creating&nbsp;
a silt deposit adjacent to the shore,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the mud will get carried farther offshore;&nbsp;
this is the sorting process in a nutshell.&nbsp;
Let’s now summarize what we’ve learned about&nbsp;
clastic rocks. Sediments that have been&nbsp;&nbsp;
transported far from their source over a long&nbsp;
period of time will be rich in quartz and clay,&nbsp;&nbsp;
well-sorted, and well-rounded. Sandstones that&nbsp;
were formed in beach environments are a great&nbsp;&nbsp;
example of this, where it is not uncommon&nbsp;
to see nearly pure quartz sand. Conversely,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sediments that have been deposited adjacent&nbsp;
to their source will be composed of various&nbsp;&nbsp;
silicate minerals, especially feldspar, and will&nbsp;
be relatively angular and poorly sorted. Breccia,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is a type of rock that commonly forms&nbsp;
from sediment that is deposited in landslides&nbsp;&nbsp;
and lahars, displays these characteristics.
We now know about sediment, where it comes from,&nbsp;&nbsp;
how its transported, and how it changes during&nbsp;
transport. All that is left to explore is how&nbsp;&nbsp;
sediment changes after it is deposited. The&nbsp;
chemical and physical changes that occur during&nbsp;&nbsp;
and after the burial of a body of sediment is&nbsp;
called diagenesis. Sediment is deposited in&nbsp;&nbsp;
basins, or low-lying areas that are typically near&nbsp;
sea level. As successive layers of sediment stack&nbsp;&nbsp;
up, their weight acts to compact the underlying&nbsp;
sediment. In a typical mud, there can be more&nbsp;&nbsp;
than 60% pore space, that is to say, 60% of the&nbsp;
volume of this mud is empty space. In sediment&nbsp;&nbsp;
that is buried in a basin, the pore space will&nbsp;
almost exclusively be filled with groundwater,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which becomes important later. The deeper a&nbsp;
sediment is buried, the more it will compact,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with a 50% reduction in porosity occurring by a&nbsp;
depth of about 2000 meters. The chemical changes&nbsp;&nbsp;
that occur during diagenesis generally involve&nbsp;
either precipitating or dissolving minerals with&nbsp;&nbsp;
groundwater. The most important chemical&nbsp;
change during diagenesis is cementation,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which involves the precipitation of minerals&nbsp;
into the pore space of a body of sediment,&nbsp;&nbsp;
transforming it into a rock. The most common&nbsp;
cement-forming minerals are quartz and calcite.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Some sediments, especially those made of carbonate&nbsp;
minerals, will recrystallize during diagenesis,&nbsp;&nbsp;
turning many small, irregular crystals into&nbsp;
larger, more equant ones. Being one of the more&nbsp;&nbsp;
soluble mineral groups, the carbonates can also&nbsp;
dissolve during diagenesis, forming large voids.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Whether cementation or dissolution occurs depends&nbsp;
on the chemistry of the sediment’s pore water. For&nbsp;&nbsp;
example, calcite tends to dissolve under acidic&nbsp;
conditions and precipitate when the pH is higher.&nbsp;
That covers the mineralogy of sedimentary rocks,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so let’s move forward and get some&nbsp;
information on their classification next.
