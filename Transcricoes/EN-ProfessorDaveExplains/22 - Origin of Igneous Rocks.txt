Now that we have a better understanding of&nbsp;
minerals, we are ready to discuss rocks.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Minerals and rocks form in response to&nbsp;
environmental forcing, with important&nbsp;&nbsp;
variables being pressure, temperature,&nbsp;
and chemistry of the starting material.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Here, we will discuss the crystallization of magma&nbsp;
and the importance of its chemistry in determining&nbsp;&nbsp;
a rock’s mineralogy. There are three types of&nbsp;
rocks: igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and these differ in their mechanism of formation.&nbsp;
We will begin our discussion with igneous rocks,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which were the first type of rocks to appear on&nbsp;
Earth after its formation and subsequent cooling.&nbsp;
Igneous rocks are formed when a mass of melted&nbsp;
rock crystallizes. There are two terms geologists&nbsp;&nbsp;
use for molten rock. When it is underground, it&nbsp;
is called magma, up here it is called lava. So,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where does this magma come from? Most igneous&nbsp;
rocks were once mantle rock that, at some point,&nbsp;&nbsp;
melted and moved toward the surface, where it&nbsp;
cooled and solidified. You may now be wondering&nbsp;&nbsp;
why lava isn’t erupting everywhere on Earth. It&nbsp;
turns out that under normal conditions, the mantle&nbsp;&nbsp;
is perfectly happy as a solid and cannot melt;&nbsp;
and volcanoes are an exception to the rule. So,&nbsp;&nbsp;
what sort of process is going on here that allows&nbsp;
the mantle to melt? In fact, there are two ways&nbsp;&nbsp;
to melt the mantle in large quantities:&nbsp;
decompression melting and flux melting.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Let’s start with decompression melting. This is&nbsp;
exactly what it sounds like, melting the mantle&nbsp;&nbsp;
by lowering its pressure. Decompression melting is&nbsp;
responsible for the volcanism above both mid-ocean&nbsp;&nbsp;
ridges and hotspots. Beneath these environments,&nbsp;
hot mantle rock is moving upward due to mantle&nbsp;&nbsp;
convection. Since hot material is less dense&nbsp;
than cold material, the weight of a column of hot&nbsp;&nbsp;
material will be less than that of cold material.&nbsp;
Therefore, at a given depth, the pressure is lower&nbsp;&nbsp;
beneath a mid-ocean ridge or hotspot than&nbsp;
it is beneath a regular piece of crust.&nbsp;
Let’s follow an ascending piece of mantle rock as&nbsp;
it rises beneath a mid-ocean ridge. At a depth of&nbsp;&nbsp;
about 30 kilometers, our piece of mantle has&nbsp;
a temperature of 1400°C. Its pressure can be&nbsp;&nbsp;
calculated using the following equation: P = ρgz,&nbsp;
where ρ is its density, g is acceleration due to&nbsp;&nbsp;
gravity, and z is the depth below Earth's surface.&nbsp;
Since ρ and g are more or less constant for this&nbsp;&nbsp;
example, the main variable affecting the pressure&nbsp;
is the depth, which should be fairly intuitive:&nbsp;&nbsp;
the more mass above a given area, the higher&nbsp;
the pressure will be. The two black arrows&nbsp;&nbsp;
are representative of the downward pressure.&nbsp;
The upper right section shows a phase diagram&nbsp;&nbsp;
corresponding to the state of our piece of mantle&nbsp;
in pressure-depth space. The green dashed curve is&nbsp;&nbsp;
the average geotherm, which shows how temperature&nbsp;
changes with depth. The red solid line is the&nbsp;&nbsp;
melting point of mantle rock. Notice how the two&nbsp;
curves are nearly parallel, this illustrates the&nbsp;&nbsp;
requirement for abnormal conditions to get the&nbsp;
mantle to melt. As our mantle rock ascends,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it experiences lower pressure, since there is&nbsp;
now less mass above it. But notice that the&nbsp;&nbsp;
temperature did not change, it is still 1400°C.&nbsp;
This is an example of an adiabatic process,&nbsp;&nbsp;
meaning heat has not been added or removed from&nbsp;
the system. Essentially, the rising motion of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the mantle occurs at sufficient speed that we can&nbsp;
assume that heat is not lost to its surroundings&nbsp;&nbsp;
during ascent. Therefore, the key to decompression&nbsp;
melting is the advection of hot material upward,&nbsp;&nbsp;
to a lower-pressure environment. Finally, at a&nbsp;
pressure of 2 kilobars, the mantle is melting,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the purple dashed line signaling the onset of&nbsp;
melting. So long as there is upward-moving mantle,&nbsp;&nbsp;
our magma factory will persist - this is&nbsp;
decompression melting. The vast majority of&nbsp;&nbsp;
volcanism on Earth, both past and present,&nbsp;
is due to decompression partial melting.&nbsp;
Flux melting involves the addition of water&nbsp;
to mantle rock, which lowers its melting&nbsp;&nbsp;
point. This is responsible for the volcanism at&nbsp;
subduction zones. Here, the subducting oceanic&nbsp;&nbsp;
lithosphere releases water into the mantle&nbsp;
above it, which induces melting. It is not&nbsp;&nbsp;
actual liquid water that is transported to the&nbsp;
mantle, but hydroxide anions that are released&nbsp;&nbsp;
from the high-pressure breakdown of hydrous&nbsp;
minerals, like serpentine, or Mg3Si2O5(OH)4.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This chemically bound water is released&nbsp;
around the same depth everywhere on Earth,&nbsp;&nbsp;
about 100 kilometers, which explains why volcanoes&nbsp;
occur in a line, or arc at subduction zones.&nbsp;
Since most igneous rocks are derived from&nbsp;
the mantle, which is relatively chemically&nbsp;&nbsp;
homogeneous, we might expect igneous rocks to also&nbsp;
be this way, but they aren’t. To understand this,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we must first discuss the crystallization process,&nbsp;
and more importantly, crystallization sequence. In&nbsp;&nbsp;
a cooling magma, crystallization order is&nbsp;
dependent upon the chemistry of the magma&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the relative melting points of the stable&nbsp;
minerals. Since most magmas are derived from&nbsp;&nbsp;
melting the same stuff, the mantle, we can ignore&nbsp;
the influence of chemistry for these purposes.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In 1928, pioneering geologist N. L.&nbsp;
Bowen created an experimental model&nbsp;&nbsp;
for the crystallization order of a standard&nbsp;
magma as it cools and chemically evolves. In&nbsp;&nbsp;
Bowen’s Reaction Series, the first mineral to&nbsp;
crystallize is olivine, followed by pyroxene,&nbsp;&nbsp;
then amphibole, then biotite, and finally&nbsp;
potassium feldspar, muscovite, and quartz.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Plagioclase can exist at all stages of Bowen’s&nbsp;
Reaction Series, as it is the most abundant&nbsp;&nbsp;
mineral in the crust. We may recall that the first&nbsp;
mineral to crystallize, olivine, an orthosilicate,&nbsp;&nbsp;
has the smallest amount of silica of all the&nbsp;
silicate minerals, while the last group contains&nbsp;&nbsp;
potassium feldspar and quartz, which are framework&nbsp;
silicates, the minerals with the most silica. This&nbsp;&nbsp;
has important implications for the composition of&nbsp;
a cooling melt. Let’s consider an average basalt,&nbsp;&nbsp;
having 50% SiO2, 15% Al2O3, 20% MgO, 10% CaO, and&nbsp;
5% alkalis. Now, let’s start to crystallize out&nbsp;&nbsp;
forsterite, the magnesium endmember of olivine,&nbsp;
which is composed of 57% MgO and 43% SiO2. What&nbsp;&nbsp;
is going to happen? Well, the olivine crystals,&nbsp;
which are more dense, will sink to the bottom&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the magma chamber, becoming isolated from&nbsp;
the upward-moving magma. Since olivine has a&nbsp;&nbsp;
smaller fraction of silica in comparison to the&nbsp;
melt, the melt will become enriched in silica&nbsp;&nbsp;
as olivine crystallizes. In fact, the melt&nbsp;
will become enriched in everything but MgO,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which gets depleted making olivine, since olivine&nbsp;
has a higher fraction of MgO than the melt. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
result is a melt that becomes enriched in silica,&nbsp;
calcium, alkalis, and aluminum as it cools and&nbsp;&nbsp;
moves upward. This process is then repeated for&nbsp;
pyroxene, amphibole, and biotite, which depletes&nbsp;&nbsp;
the melt in everything but silica, aluminum, and&nbsp;
the alkalis, which become concentrated in the&nbsp;&nbsp;
cooling melt. This results in the last minerals to&nbsp;
form being those with silicon, aluminum, sodium,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and potassium in their chemistry, such as&nbsp;
quartz, muscovite, and alkali feldspar.&nbsp;
Solid solution further complicates things. For a&nbsp;
general rule of thumb, magnesium-rich endmembers&nbsp;&nbsp;
crystallize before iron-rich endmembers,&nbsp;
and calcium-rich endmembers form before&nbsp;&nbsp;
sodium-rich endmembers. This explains the&nbsp;
right side of Bowen’s Reaction Series;&nbsp;&nbsp;
since anorthite has a higher melting point than&nbsp;
albite, the first plagioclase that crystallizes&nbsp;&nbsp;
will be calcium-rich and the last will be more&nbsp;
sodium-rich. The same thing happens with olivine,&nbsp;&nbsp;
pyroxene, and amphibole; the first minerals&nbsp;
to form will be magnesium-rich, with crystals&nbsp;&nbsp;
becoming progressively iron-enriched during&nbsp;
cooling. These processes are together referred&nbsp;&nbsp;
to as differentiation, which describes how a&nbsp;
magma changes, or becomes different, as it cools.&nbsp;
The amount of differentiation a magma&nbsp;
undergoes is generally related to the&nbsp;&nbsp;
amount of crust that it had to move through to&nbsp;
make it near the surface. Therefore, the coolest&nbsp;&nbsp;
and most differentiated magmas commonly erupt&nbsp;
in areas where the crust is relatively thick,&nbsp;&nbsp;
such as on continents. This is easy to see when&nbsp;
comparing Hawaii and Yellowstone, as they are both&nbsp;&nbsp;
hotspots. The volcanoes of Hawaii erupt basaltic,&nbsp;
or mafic lava at around 1300 degrees Celsius,&nbsp;&nbsp;
whereas Yellowstone erupts cool, felsic magma&nbsp;
that is so thick and viscous, that it clogs up the&nbsp;&nbsp;
volcano, causing extremely explosive eruptions.&nbsp;
What is the difference? The Yellowstone hotspot&nbsp;&nbsp;
is beneath thick continental crust, while the&nbsp;
Hawaii hotspot is beneath thin, oceanic crust. We&nbsp;&nbsp;
have more to discuss regarding igneous rocks, so&nbsp;
let’s move forward and learn more about them now.
