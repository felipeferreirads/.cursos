We just learned about some different types of&nbsp;
physical and chemical weathering processes. But&nbsp;&nbsp;
we should also understand that weathering&nbsp;
occurs in a wide variety of environments,&nbsp;&nbsp;
producing unique characteristics in the&nbsp;
sedimentary rocks that eventually form&nbsp;&nbsp;
there. We already discussed sediments on the ocean&nbsp;
floor when we discussed the geology of the ocean&nbsp;&nbsp;
floor. On land, the most significant weathering&nbsp;
environments are fluvial, aeolian, and glacial.&nbsp;
Before we get started, it is important to&nbsp;
understand that any weathering environment&nbsp;&nbsp;
can be either depositional or erosional,&nbsp;
depending on its energy. High energy&nbsp;&nbsp;
environments tend to be erosional, and&nbsp;
low energy environments are depositional.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Sediment can be transported by water, wind,&nbsp;
and ice. Moreover, environments dominated&nbsp;&nbsp;
by water are called fluvial, by wind are called&nbsp;
aeolian, and by ice are called glacial. Glacial&nbsp;&nbsp;
environments are almost always high energy because&nbsp;
of the tremendous pressures found beneath them,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where they are in contact with rocks and sediment.&nbsp;
The energy of an aeolian environment is a function&nbsp;&nbsp;
of its average wind velocity. And in fluvial&nbsp;
environments, its average water velocity.&nbsp;
Let’s begin by looking at fluvial environments,&nbsp;
which are most common. With these, the water&nbsp;&nbsp;
velocity is a determined by the slope of the land.&nbsp;
Streams begin in the areas of highest elevation,&nbsp;&nbsp;
growing and slowing as they approach the sea,&nbsp;
running through progressively flatter land.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Therefore, fluvial environments start out as&nbsp;
erosional and become more and more depositional&nbsp;&nbsp;
as they near the ocean. Though each environment&nbsp;
is different, they all effectively move sediment&nbsp;&nbsp;
from high elevation to low elevation.
Streams are channeled, flowing water&nbsp;&nbsp;
bodies that transport precipitation and&nbsp;
glacial meltwater to basins, or low-lying,&nbsp;&nbsp;
bowl-shaped areas in Earth’s crust. Though some&nbsp;
streams transport water to lakes, all of Earth’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
surface water that doesn’t evaporate is eventually&nbsp;
transported to the oceans. Streams form large,&nbsp;&nbsp;
interconnected networks, called drainage basins,&nbsp;
that are delineated by local maxima in elevation.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For example, the Mississippi River Basin&nbsp;
is a network of streams and rivers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
east of the Rockies and west of the Appalachians,&nbsp;
all of which drain into the Gulf of Mexico through&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Mississippi River. This river system is&nbsp;
overall erosional, only beginning to form&nbsp;&nbsp;
large sedimentary deposits downstream of the area&nbsp;
where the Ohio and Mississippi rivers converge.&nbsp;
Let’s now talk about how sediment is transported&nbsp;
in streams to better understand how it is&nbsp;&nbsp;
deposited. In an earlier tutorial on sedimentary&nbsp;
rocks, we learned that the ability of a stream&nbsp;&nbsp;
to transport a sediment depends on the diameter&nbsp;
of the sediment and the velocity of the stream,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the coarsest sediment requiring the highest&nbsp;
water velocities to be eroded and transported.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In streams, the slowest water flow occurs at&nbsp;
the bottom of the bed, due to friction between&nbsp;&nbsp;
the stream bed and the gravity-driven water. This&nbsp;
allows large sediments like boulders and pebbles&nbsp;&nbsp;
to settle and be deposited at the bottom. If the&nbsp;
water flow suddenly increases, say during a large&nbsp;&nbsp;
storm, the sediment at the bottom, beginning with&nbsp;
the finest-grained material, say fine sand, can&nbsp;&nbsp;
bounce off other rocks and saltate, or jump, into&nbsp;
the stream’s flow and move further downstream. If&nbsp;&nbsp;
the stream velocity further increases, the&nbsp;
saltating fine sand will become suspended in&nbsp;&nbsp;
the stream and the next coarsest material, medium&nbsp;
sand, will begin to saltate. Later when the storm&nbsp;&nbsp;
is over and the flow wanes, the sand will stop&nbsp;
moving and be deposited at the stream bottom in a&nbsp;&nbsp;
new location further downstream. In high-gradient&nbsp;
streams, larger sediment like boulders,&nbsp;&nbsp;
are pushed or rolled downstream by the rapidly&nbsp;
flowing water through a process called traction.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Most streams have a sufficiently high velocity&nbsp;
that the muddy sediments, like silt and clay,&nbsp;&nbsp;
are always suspended and are not deposited in the&nbsp;
channel; however, when a stream spills its banks,&nbsp;&nbsp;
muddy sediment is deposited in the flood plain,&nbsp;
or low-lying areas surrounding the stream.&nbsp;
Saltation and traction both cause sediments&nbsp;
to bash into one another and break down&nbsp;&nbsp;
into smaller pieces, sometimes being broken&nbsp;
small enough to become suspended in the flow.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Swedish geographer, Filip Hjulstrӧm,&nbsp;
discovered a relationship between sediment size,&nbsp;&nbsp;
stream flow velocity, and whether the sediment&nbsp;
would be transported, deposited, or eroded.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For example, a millimeter-sized grain of sand will&nbsp;
remain deposited on the stream bed until the flow&nbsp;&nbsp;
speed exceeds 10 centimeters per second, and the&nbsp;
grain begins to saltate. As long as the flow speed&nbsp;&nbsp;
stays above 10 centimeters per second, the sand&nbsp;
grain will continue to saltate downstream. With&nbsp;&nbsp;
respect to weathering, the most impactful changes&nbsp;
made to sediments in fluvial environments is their&nbsp;&nbsp;
rounding, sorting, and reduction in grain&nbsp;
size. In addition, the chemical weathering&nbsp;&nbsp;
processes that we discussed in the previous&nbsp;
tutorial operate in fluvial environments.&nbsp;
We can discuss fluvial processes outside of the&nbsp;
context of a stream as well. Slope failures,&nbsp;&nbsp;
also called mass wasting, refer to the&nbsp;
movement of sediment downhill under the&nbsp;&nbsp;
force of gravity. Mass wasting primarily&nbsp;
occurs in areas with high relief, such as&nbsp;&nbsp;
jagged mountain peaks or fault escarpments. Mass&nbsp;
wasting can be broken down into two main types:&nbsp;&nbsp;
creep, which is slow and steady movement, and&nbsp;
landslides, which are sudden and catastrophic.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Creep, or soil creep, is a process where soil&nbsp;
slowly slides downhill over the bedrock. Small&nbsp;&nbsp;
changes in particle volume that occur during&nbsp;
freeze-thaw cycles or diurnal temperature&nbsp;&nbsp;
changes break down soil cohesiveness, causing&nbsp;
very small movements that accumulate over time.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Landslides are rapid movements of soil and rock&nbsp;
downslope under the force of gravity. Landslides&nbsp;&nbsp;
are usually initiated by some event, like an&nbsp;
earthquake, recent deforestation, or a heavy&nbsp;&nbsp;
precipitation event. There are different types of&nbsp;
landslides that are categorized based on the type&nbsp;&nbsp;
of sediment in the slide and the sediment’s water&nbsp;
content. Rockfalls involve pieces of bedrock that&nbsp;&nbsp;
break off from a cliff face, usually along joints,&nbsp;
and fall down to stable ground forming a large,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sloped pile called a talus slope. Water is&nbsp;
not very important to rockfalls. The other two&nbsp;&nbsp;
types of landslides, debris flows and mud flows,&nbsp;
behave more like fluids, due to higher amounts of&nbsp;&nbsp;
water-rich mud greasing up the flow. Earthquakes&nbsp;
are often accompanied by mass wasting events. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
agitation of water-saturated sediments by seismic&nbsp;
waves causes a loss of cohesion, allowing solid&nbsp;&nbsp;
materials to behave more like fluids. This process&nbsp;
is called liquefaction, and can cause great&nbsp;&nbsp;
damage to buildings and other structures built on&nbsp;
unconsolidated materials, even on flat-lying land.&nbsp;
Debris flows are masses of soil, rock, and&nbsp;
often tree limbs that flow down mountainsides,&nbsp;&nbsp;
eventually following drainage patterns into&nbsp;
streams and continuing to flow downstream&nbsp;&nbsp;
until the slope becomes too shallow and they run&nbsp;
out of gravitational potential energy. Mud flows&nbsp;&nbsp;
are like debris flows, but muddier and therefore&nbsp;
better-flowing. Lahars are a type of mud flow&nbsp;&nbsp;
where a volcanic eruption triggers a rapid melting&nbsp;
of snow and glacial ice, which mixes with volcanic&nbsp;&nbsp;
sediments and flows down the mountain. Mud and&nbsp;
debris flows have the consistency of wet concrete,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and due to their high density, are excellent&nbsp;
at entraining more and more sediment as they&nbsp;&nbsp;
move along. This also makes them extremely&nbsp;
dangerous, as a mud flow of any depth can&nbsp;&nbsp;
easily sweep away a human or even a car.&nbsp;
Lahars are actually the greatest cause of&nbsp;&nbsp;
death during volcanic eruptions. During the 1985&nbsp;
eruption of Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia, about&nbsp;&nbsp;
25,000 people died in towns 46 kilometers from the&nbsp;
volcano as lahars overran and buried their towns.&nbsp;
With fluvial processes covered,&nbsp;&nbsp;
that brings us to aeolian processes,&nbsp;
so let’s learn about those next.
