We are making our way through the Phanerozoic Eon,&nbsp;
and we just learned about the first era within,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Paleozoic Era. Let’s pick things back&nbsp;
up right as this era was coming to a close.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Trouble was brewing in Siberia around 250&nbsp;
million years ago. As convection currents&nbsp;&nbsp;
within the Earth’s mantle changed, a mantle&nbsp;
plume was forming. Mantle plumes are hot,&nbsp;&nbsp;
rising areas of Earth’s mantle and are responsible&nbsp;
for some of the largest volcanic eruptions.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Siberian Traps erupted around 4 million cubic&nbsp;
kilometers of lava within only two million years,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is comparable to the entire&nbsp;
Mediterranean Sea being filled with lava.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Not only did this volcanism spew large amounts&nbsp;
of CO2 into the atmosphere, but the volcanoes&nbsp;&nbsp;
erupted in the vicinity of large deposits&nbsp;
of coal and carbonate rocks. The heat from&nbsp;&nbsp;
the volcanoes burned these carbonaceous rocks,&nbsp;
which released even more CO2 into the atmosphere.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This huge outpouring of greenhouse gas into the&nbsp;
atmosphere caused significant global warming,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which along with other factors led to widespread&nbsp;
ocean anoxia and acidification, causing the&nbsp;&nbsp;
extinction of over 90% of all species. This was&nbsp;
the largest mass extinction event of all time and&nbsp;&nbsp;
is known as the “Great Dying”. Only time will tell&nbsp;
if an event of such magnitude will be repeated.&nbsp;
After the end-Permian mass extinction, life&nbsp;
immediately began to recover and evolution&nbsp;&nbsp;
kicked into high gear, taking advantage of&nbsp;
increased living space and reduction of predators.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Thus began the Mesozoic Era, which&nbsp;
is known as the age of the dinosaurs,&nbsp;&nbsp;
since they evolved and subsequently&nbsp;
took over Earth during this time.&nbsp;&nbsp;
In addition to the evolution of dinosaurs,&nbsp;
which occurred around 240 million years ago,&nbsp;&nbsp;
mammals also evolved during the Mesozoic,&nbsp;
but around 30 million years later.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This evolutionary head start helped the dinosaurs&nbsp;
dominate the mammals throughout their existence.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For a long time, dinosaurs were imagined&nbsp;
to be enormous, lumbering creatures,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but recent discoveries suggest that they may have&nbsp;
been warm-blooded, meaning they could raise their&nbsp;&nbsp;
body temperature in cold weather, allowing then to&nbsp;
be more active than any other animal at that time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
at least until the evolution of mammals.
The most impactful tectonic event of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
Mesozoic Era was the breaking apart of Pangea,&nbsp;
which began around 180 million years ago. The axis&nbsp;&nbsp;
along which Pangea broke apart would go on to&nbsp;
become the Atlantic Ocean, as the crust was&nbsp;&nbsp;
stretched and thinned down to below sea level due&nbsp;
to the divergent motions of the mantle below. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
Atlantic Ocean continues to widen to this day as&nbsp;
new oceanic crust is created along its axis, where&nbsp;&nbsp;
hot, upwelling mantle rock partially melts, and&nbsp;
solidifies near the surface of the ocean floor.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Along the western coast of North America,&nbsp;
which was also the western coast of Pangea,&nbsp;&nbsp;
convergent forces dominated, and a new subduction&nbsp;
zone was created around 200 million years ago.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This would begin a theme of continuing&nbsp;
cycles of mountain building and volcanism&nbsp;&nbsp;
that have also lasted until present day. This&nbsp;
convergent plate boundary was responsible&nbsp;&nbsp;
for uplifting the Rocky Mountains, which&nbsp;
began forming around 80 million years ago.&nbsp;&nbsp;
As we wrap up the Mesozoic Era, some&nbsp;
extraterrestrial trouble is on the way that&nbsp;&nbsp;
will change the Earth’s biosphere forever.
The Mesozoic Era ended with another mass&nbsp;&nbsp;
extinction event, but this one was not of&nbsp;
an Earthly origin. An asteroid with a mass&nbsp;&nbsp;
equivalent to about 50 million Titanics slammed&nbsp;
into Earth near the present-day Yucatan Peninsula.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The impact itself was equivalent to a 100 million&nbsp;
megaton bomb. The thermal pulse from this impact&nbsp;&nbsp;
instantly killed anything nearby and the shockwave&nbsp;
created hurricane force winds extending out about&nbsp;&nbsp;
1500 kilometers from the blast site. It was&nbsp;
a seismic event equivalent to a magnitude 10&nbsp;&nbsp;
earthquake. Because the asteroid impacted the&nbsp;
ocean, tsunamis 100 to 300 meters high radiated&nbsp;&nbsp;
across the Atlantic, crashing along coastlines,&nbsp;
and penetrating up to 100 kilometers inland.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Molten fragments of Earth were ejected&nbsp;
into the atmosphere upon impact that,&nbsp;&nbsp;
upon returning to the surface, would heat Earth’s&nbsp;
surface to hundreds of degrees Fahrenheit,&nbsp;&nbsp;
devastating terrestrial plants and animals, but&nbsp;
sparing most marine life due to the high heat&nbsp;&nbsp;
capacity of water. The combination of these events&nbsp;
obliterated life proximal to the impact site,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but the real worldwide devastation&nbsp;
would come from long term consequences.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Dust particles were blown into the&nbsp;
atmosphere, where they remained for months.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This blocked off sunlight, causing both perpetual&nbsp;
darkness and a global winter. This several month&nbsp;&nbsp;
period of darkness would have likely killed off&nbsp;
anything that relied on photosynthesis for energy,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which would then travel down the food&nbsp;
chain as herbivores ran out of food.&nbsp;
However, the longest-term effect of the impact was&nbsp;
the global warming associated with CO2 released&nbsp;&nbsp;
from vaporized evaporite rocks, which precipitated&nbsp;
from the evaporation of ocean water. These rocks&nbsp;&nbsp;
were primarily composed of calcium carbonate, the&nbsp;
source for the CO2, and calcium sulfate, which&nbsp;&nbsp;
vaporized and reacted with precipitation to form&nbsp;
sulfuric acid, which rained down upon the Earth.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The released CO2 was the main long-term problem,&nbsp;
raising Earth’s temperature by around 7 degrees&nbsp;&nbsp;
Celsius for a few hundred thousand years.&nbsp;
In addition, the rapid transition from&nbsp;&nbsp;
impact-induced cooling to warming would&nbsp;
have been very difficult for most types of&nbsp;&nbsp;
life to deal with. As a result of the events just&nbsp;
described, around 80% of all species went extinct,&nbsp;&nbsp;
including all dinosaurs. Though the loss was&nbsp;
great, events at the end of the Cretaceous&nbsp;&nbsp;
Period opened the door for a renewal of life and&nbsp;
a mammal-dominated world in the Cenozoic Era.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Let’s move forward and wrap up our&nbsp;
journey with this third and final era.
