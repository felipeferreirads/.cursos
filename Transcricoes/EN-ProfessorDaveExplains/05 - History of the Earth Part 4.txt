At this point we have covered the Hadean,&nbsp;
Archean, and Proterozoic Eons, and we are&nbsp;&nbsp;
working our way through the Phanerozoic Eon. We&nbsp;
made it through the Paleozoic and Mesozoic Eras,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which brings us to the third and final era of&nbsp;
this eon, which is called the Cenozoic Era.&nbsp;
The Cenozoic Era is the period in Earth’s history&nbsp;
spanning from 66 million years ago to the present.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The Cenozoic Era is called the “Age of&nbsp;
Mammals” due to their rapid and intense&nbsp;&nbsp;
radiation, thanks to the end-Cretaceous mass&nbsp;
extinction which killed off the dinosaurs.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The early mammals were small creatures, ranging&nbsp;
from the size of small rodents to medium-sized&nbsp;&nbsp;
dogs. The first primate, Altiatlasius, evolved&nbsp;
around 57 million years ago in North Africa.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Primates, like other mammals, experienced&nbsp;
rapid radiation in the Cenozoic Era.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Types of primates include lemurs, gorillas,&nbsp;
chimpanzees, and humans. The first member of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the human genus, Homo, evolved around 2.4 million&nbsp;
years ago from Australopithecus. Homo sapiens,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or humans, emerged between 200,000&nbsp;
and 300,000 years ago in Africa.&nbsp;&nbsp;
But this fascinating story will have to be&nbsp;
saved for the upcoming anthropology series.&nbsp;
By the Cenozoic Era, most of Earth’s&nbsp;
continents were near their current locations,&nbsp;&nbsp;
except for India. The Indian continent was making&nbsp;
a beeline toward Asia during the early Cenozoic,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with collision and orogeny occurring&nbsp;
around 50 million years ago.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This event initiated uplift of the Himalayan&nbsp;
Mountains and is still occurring to this day.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Additional smaller changes in geography greatly&nbsp;
impacted the Cenozoic climate. For example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Antarctica became an isolated continent around 34&nbsp;
million years ago as Australia and South America&nbsp;&nbsp;
broke away, moving northward. This led to the&nbsp;
development of a cold ocean current around&nbsp;&nbsp;
Antarctica, helping trap colder air over the&nbsp;
continent. The development of the so-called&nbsp;&nbsp;
circumpolar current led to global cooling and&nbsp;
the development of the first permanent Antarctic&nbsp;&nbsp;
ice sheet, marking the beginning of the modern&nbsp;
ice age. The global cooling was caused by the&nbsp;&nbsp;
development of a robust oceanic circulation, where&nbsp;
the cold, dense water of the circumpolar current&nbsp;&nbsp;
sinks down and spreads out. This led to the&nbsp;
cooling of the lower ocean, which set the stage&nbsp;&nbsp;
for more atmospheric CO2 to be stored as sediment&nbsp;
on the ocean floor, reducing greenhouse warming.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The next major change was the development&nbsp;
of glaciers in the Northern Hemisphere,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which existed by around 3.2 million years&nbsp;
ago. The cause of this is still debated,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it was probably related to the&nbsp;
closing of the Isthmus of Panama,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which occurred around the same time. The Pacific&nbsp;
and Atlantic Oceans used to be connected where&nbsp;&nbsp;
Panama is today, which allowed their waters&nbsp;
to mix. Since the closing of the isthmus,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the Atlantic Ocean has become much saltier, which&nbsp;
initiated a reorganization of ocean circulation.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Gulf stream waters stopped circulating through&nbsp;
the Arctic Ocean, instead sinking to the seafloor&nbsp;&nbsp;
around Greenland and spreading out. This shut off&nbsp;
the transport of warm water to the Arctic Ocean,&nbsp;&nbsp;
causing the surrounding regions of Greenland,&nbsp;
Canada, and Northern Europe to cool and develop&nbsp;&nbsp;
permanent ice sheets. We have been living in&nbsp;
this northern hemisphere ice age ever since.&nbsp;
Within an ice age, there are periods of time&nbsp;
called glacial periods where glaciers expand&nbsp;&nbsp;
and interglacial periods where glaciers&nbsp;
retreat. Fluctuations in climate due to&nbsp;&nbsp;
changes in Earth’s orbit are responsible for&nbsp;
glacial cycles. These changes in Earth’s orbit,&nbsp;&nbsp;
called Milankovitch Cycles, include precession,&nbsp;
or the change in the direction that Earth’s&nbsp;&nbsp;
north pole points, obliquity, the&nbsp;
change in the angle of Earth’s axis,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and eccentricity, the change in&nbsp;
the ellipticity of Earth’s orbit.&nbsp;&nbsp;
They operate on timescales from tens&nbsp;
to hundreds of thousands of years.&nbsp;&nbsp;
We are currently in an interglacial period with&nbsp;
the prospect of expanding glaciers in sight,&nbsp;&nbsp;
at least according to the Milankovitch Cycles.&nbsp;
We are instead barreling toward a subtropical&nbsp;&nbsp;
arctic with no ice sheets, due to the global&nbsp;
warming caused by burning fossil fuels.&nbsp;&nbsp;
More on glaciers later in the series.

with the timeline describing the history of&nbsp;
