All the way at the beginning of this geology&nbsp;
series, we talked about Earth’s history and&nbsp;&nbsp;
the geological timescale spanning&nbsp;
4.5 billion years of Earthly events,&nbsp;&nbsp;
from the Hadean eon to the Phanerozoic eon&nbsp;
we are still living in today. But how is it&nbsp;&nbsp;
that geologists determine the age of the&nbsp;
Earth and its different rock formations?&nbsp;
There are two methods of dating rocks. These&nbsp;
are relative dating, which qualitatively&nbsp;&nbsp;
compares the age of formations based on their&nbsp;
stratigraphic sequence, and absolute dating,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or radiometric dating, which uses the decay of&nbsp;
radioisotopes to calculate a rock’s precise age.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Before the advent of radiometric dating in the&nbsp;
early 1900s, geologists assigned relative dates&nbsp;&nbsp;
to different layers in an outcrop based on&nbsp;
the inferred sequence of their deposition,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or emplacement, which they would then compare to&nbsp;
other outcrops using distinctive formations called&nbsp;&nbsp;
marker beds. In fact, most sedimentary rocks&nbsp;
are dated using relative dating since they do&nbsp;&nbsp;
not meet the criteria for radiometric dating,&nbsp;
which we will discuss in the next tutorial.&nbsp;
Let’s now discuss some of the principles that&nbsp;
geologists use when determining relative ages. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
principle of original horizontality states that&nbsp;
sediments accumulate in horizontal layers called&nbsp;&nbsp;
beds, though some sand layers that are deposited&nbsp;
as dunes may be inclined as much as 35 degrees.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This is called crossbedding. In&nbsp;
cross bedded sedimentary rocks,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the sediment is deposited in sets at the angle&nbsp;
of repose on the lee side of dunes and ripples,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which explains their inclination. In addition,&nbsp;
only sand-sized sediment can form cross beds.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Say for example that you find an outcrop with&nbsp;
a horizontally layered siltstone on top of a&nbsp;&nbsp;
vertically layered siltstone. When you apply the&nbsp;
principle of original horizontality, you realize&nbsp;&nbsp;
that there was a large time gap between deposition&nbsp;
of the horizontal and vertical formations.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The principle of superposition states that,&nbsp;
unless tectonic forces have overturned the&nbsp;&nbsp;
outcrop, beds on the bottom are&nbsp;
usually older than beds on top.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The principle of cross-cutting relationships&nbsp;
states that any geological feature which cuts&nbsp;&nbsp;
across a rock must be younger than the feature&nbsp;
it disrupts. So, if an igneous intrusion is found&nbsp;&nbsp;
cutting through a sandstone, the intrusion must&nbsp;
be younger than the sandstone it cuts through.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The principle of inclusions states that any&nbsp;
rock fragments that are a part of a larger&nbsp;&nbsp;
formation must be older than the formation they&nbsp;
are a part of. For example, lithic fragments,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are pieces of a preexisting rock, are common&nbsp;
types of grains in sedimentary rocks; so if you&nbsp;&nbsp;
find a clast of schist in a sandstone, the schist&nbsp;
must be older than the sandstone it is a part of.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The principle of faunal succession states that&nbsp;
there is a historical order in which organisms&nbsp;&nbsp;
evolved over time, and that certain specific&nbsp;
fossils can be used to determine a rock’s age.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Organisms that only existed for a short period&nbsp;
of Earth’s history are most useful for this,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and their fossils are called index fossils.&nbsp;
For example, if you find a trilobite fossil&nbsp;&nbsp;
in a bed, then it must have been deposited&nbsp;
between the Cambrian and Permian Periods.&nbsp;
Another useful concept for qualitatively dating&nbsp;
rocks is that of “missing time”, or gaps in the&nbsp;&nbsp;
rock record called unconformities. There are&nbsp;
four types of unconformities: a nonconformity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
an angular unconformity, a disconformity, and a&nbsp;
paraconformity, all of which represent missing&nbsp;&nbsp;
time. But what exactly is meant by missing time?&nbsp;
Recall the Wilson cycle from a previous tutorial,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the ways that geologic environments change&nbsp;
over time. For example, during one period an area&nbsp;&nbsp;
may be a part of a sedimentary basin, but then&nbsp;
get uplifted 100 million years later during an&nbsp;&nbsp;
orogeny, transforming the once sedimentary&nbsp;
environment into an erosional environment,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and then, after another 100 million years, it&nbsp;
could once again become a sedimentary basin.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Let’s consider what the rock record would look&nbsp;
like here. Sedimentary rocks would be deposited&nbsp;&nbsp;
during the first period, which would later&nbsp;
get eroded during uplift, removing some amount&nbsp;&nbsp;
of the sedimentary record, which would later&nbsp;
be capped by sediments from the last period.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The rocks that were eroded during&nbsp;
uplift represent missing time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the gap between the two sedimentary&nbsp;
layers is called an unconformity.&nbsp;
Let’s rigorously define the types of&nbsp;
unconformities. A disconformity is an erosional&nbsp;&nbsp;
boundary between two beds of sedimentary rock, as&nbsp;
in the example we just discussed. A paraconformity&nbsp;&nbsp;
is also a boundary between two beds of sedimentary&nbsp;
rocks, but is not erosional and simply represents&nbsp;&nbsp;
a period of nondeposition. They represent less&nbsp;
missing time than a disconformity. A nonconformity&nbsp;&nbsp;
is a boundary between an older non-sedimentary&nbsp;
rock, like an igneous or metamorphic rock,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and younger sedimentary rock layered on top. And&nbsp;
an angular unconformity is a boundary between&nbsp;&nbsp;
tectonically tilted layers of sedimentary&nbsp;
rocks and overlying horizontal strata.&nbsp;
So, that covers the principles that geologists use&nbsp;
to assign relative dates to Earth’s rock layers,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and some of the challenges that&nbsp;
this method poses. As we mentioned,&nbsp;&nbsp;
prior to the development of radiometric dating,&nbsp;
this was all that geologists were able to do.&nbsp;&nbsp;
But radiometric dating is a powerful technique,&nbsp;
so let’s move forward and learn about that next.
