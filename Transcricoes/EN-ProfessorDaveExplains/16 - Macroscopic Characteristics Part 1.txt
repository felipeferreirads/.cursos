In the previous tutorial, we talked about&nbsp;
minerals and the different types of bonding&nbsp;&nbsp;
they can exhibit. Now we are going to&nbsp;
take that knowledge and expand upon it&nbsp;&nbsp;
so that you can start identifying minerals in the&nbsp;
wild. Geologists start with one simple property to&nbsp;&nbsp;
identify minerals by eye: luster. Luster describes&nbsp;
the way light reflects off a mineral’s surface.&nbsp;&nbsp;
A mineral’s luster should first be&nbsp;
grouped into one of two categories:&nbsp;&nbsp;
metallic or non-metallic. Luster is related to&nbsp;
the speed that light travels through a mineral,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or its refractive index, and minerals that&nbsp;
transmit light slowly, with a refractive index&nbsp;&nbsp;
greater than 3, generally have a metallic luster.&nbsp;
Metallic minerals are opaque with a mirror-like,&nbsp;&nbsp;
shiny luster, while non-metallic minerals,&nbsp;
which can range from opaque to transparent,&nbsp;&nbsp;
tend to be less shiny than metallic minerals.&nbsp;
There are seven types of non-metallic lusters:&nbsp;&nbsp;
adamantine, vitreous, pearly,&nbsp;
silky, resinous, greasy, and dull.&nbsp;
Minerals with adamantine luster are transparent&nbsp;
to translucent and are the shiniest of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
non-metallics. Diamonds, with their high&nbsp;
refractive index, are the most well-known example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but other minerals, like Herkimer diamonds, a&nbsp;
particularly shiny type of quartz from upstate New&nbsp;&nbsp;
York, as well as the popular diamond knock-off,&nbsp;
cubic zirconium, also get a chance to shine.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Vitreous luster looks like a&nbsp;
freshly cleaned pane of glass.&nbsp;&nbsp;
These minerals are transparent to&nbsp;
translucent and have a low refractive index.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Some of the more common minerals such as&nbsp;
quartz and calcite have a vitreous luster.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Pearly luster resembles the subtle iridescence,&nbsp;
or play of colors, found in natural pearls.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Iridescence occurs when light reflects and&nbsp;
refracts off different crystallographic planes&nbsp;&nbsp;
as it passes through a crystal. The merging of two&nbsp;
or more light waves that have been reflected off&nbsp;&nbsp;
atomic planes creates a new light wave with&nbsp;
a new and distinct waveform. Because the&nbsp;&nbsp;
distance that a light wave travels between two&nbsp;
layers is a function of the angle of incidence,&nbsp;&nbsp;
changing it will result in different wave&nbsp;
combinations, creating a shifting rainbow effect,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where the color reflected changes&nbsp;
based on the angle of observation.&nbsp;&nbsp;
It makes sense then, that minerals with a pearly&nbsp;
luster consist of transparent, coplanar sheets.&nbsp;&nbsp;
A common mineral with a pearly luster is&nbsp;
muscovite, which is sometimes used as glitter.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Minerals with a silky luster are composed of thin,&nbsp;
parallel fibers, making them reminiscent of silk.&nbsp;&nbsp;
If these “fibers” are particularly coarse,&nbsp;
more like twine then silk, it is said to have&nbsp;&nbsp;
a fibrous luster. Ulexite, a rare hydrated&nbsp;
borate, is an excellent example of silky&nbsp;&nbsp;
luster. Another example is a variety of gypsum,&nbsp;
a mineral composed of hydrated calcium sulfate,&nbsp;&nbsp;
called satin spar. Resinous luster resembles&nbsp;
hardened maple syrup or frozen pine resin.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Sphalerite, which is named after the Greek word&nbsp;
for deceptive, due to its many different crystal&nbsp;&nbsp;
appearances, can sometimes be found in nature&nbsp;
with a resinous luster, along with metallic,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or even adamantine lusters. Minerals&nbsp;
with a greasy luster appear as though&nbsp;&nbsp;
they are covered in a thin layer of oil, and many&nbsp;
minerals with a greasy luster also feel greasy.&nbsp;&nbsp;
An excellent example of this is graphite.&nbsp;
Comprised of sp2 hybridized carbon atoms forming&nbsp;&nbsp;
an extended pi system, it both has a greasy&nbsp;
luster and feels greasy. Minerals with a dull,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or earthy luster are the least shiny of them all,&nbsp;
as light is primarily scattered at the surface&nbsp;&nbsp;
instead of reflected. The clay mineral kaolinite&nbsp;
is an example of a mineral with a dull luster.&nbsp;
Color, one of the most obvious properties of a&nbsp;
mineral, is also the easiest to misinterpret.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Minerals typically get their coloration from&nbsp;
the presence of transition metals. Due to&nbsp;&nbsp;
their partially filled d-orbitals, transition&nbsp;
metals are very efficient at absorbing specific&nbsp;&nbsp;
wavelengths of visible light, which affects the&nbsp;
color that is reflected off a mineral’s surface&nbsp;&nbsp;
into your eye. Allochromatic minerals only contain&nbsp;
trace amounts of the transition metals. Corundum,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Al2O3, is colorless until about one percent of&nbsp;
the aluminum ions are replaced with a transition&nbsp;&nbsp;
metal, such as iron, titanium, or chromium.&nbsp;
Corundum with trace amounts of iron or titanium&nbsp;&nbsp;
is blue in color and is called sapphire, whereas&nbsp;
corundum with trace amounts of chromium is red,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and is called ruby. Transition metals do not&nbsp;
color all minerals identically. For example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
chromium makes rubies red, but&nbsp;
it also makes emeralds green.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Idiochromatic minerals are “self-colored”&nbsp;
by the presence of a transition metal in&nbsp;&nbsp;
their standard chemical formula. The carbonate&nbsp;
mineral malachite appears bright green due to&nbsp;&nbsp;
the absorption of red light by copper ions, which&nbsp;
makes up over half of its chemical composition.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Other sources of color include structural defects&nbsp;
in the crystal lattice. The mineral fluorite&nbsp;&nbsp;
gets its purple color from Frenkel defects, which&nbsp;
occur when a small ion slips out of its position,&nbsp;&nbsp;
leaving an electron behind in its hole,&nbsp;
which can absorb and reemit light.&nbsp;
One of the ways that color can be&nbsp;
useful for identifying a mineral&nbsp;&nbsp;
is by observing its color in a powdered form,&nbsp;
which is done by a streak test. The streak is&nbsp;&nbsp;
the powdered residue left behind after rubbing&nbsp;
a mineral on a piece of unglazed porcelain.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The streak of a mineral is much more&nbsp;
consistent than its macroscopic color.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This is because when a non-opaque material is&nbsp;
finely powdered, most of the light is reflected&nbsp;&nbsp;
off its surface, rather than being transmitted&nbsp;
and subsequently absorbed by its electrons.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Most non-metallic minerals have a white streak,&nbsp;
while metallic minerals tend to have dark-colored&nbsp;&nbsp;
streaks. For the ferric oxide mineral hematite,&nbsp;
the streak test is one of the quickest ways to&nbsp;&nbsp;
identify it. Although it can appear black, brown,&nbsp;
or even silver in color, its streak will always be&nbsp;&nbsp;
a reddish brown. Similarly, calcite, which can&nbsp;
appear in a range of colors from pink to blue,&nbsp;&nbsp;
always has a white streak. If you want a&nbsp;
way to tell the difference between pyrite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
also known as fool’s gold, and real gold,&nbsp;
pyrite always has a dark gray streak,&nbsp;&nbsp;
while gold has a pale-yellow streak.
That covers two very important macroscopic&nbsp;&nbsp;
characteristics of minerals, luster and color.&nbsp;
Let’s move forward and learn about a couple more.
