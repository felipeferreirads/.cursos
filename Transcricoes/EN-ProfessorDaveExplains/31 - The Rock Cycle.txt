Over the past several tutorials, we have&nbsp;
been learning a lot about rocks. We have&nbsp;&nbsp;
learned about the types of rocks, those being&nbsp;
igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic. We have&nbsp;&nbsp;
learned about their origins, their mineralogy,&nbsp;
and their properties. With all three types of&nbsp;&nbsp;
rocks now understood, we are ready to examine&nbsp;
something called the rock cycle, a construct&nbsp;&nbsp;
which magnificently encapsulates a huge breadth&nbsp;
of geological phenomena. All three types of rocks&nbsp;&nbsp;
are slowly changed from one type to another in&nbsp;
this process which we refer to as the rock cycle.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This involves the formation and destruction of&nbsp;
rock throughout geologic history. For example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
in Earth’s early history, all rocks at the surface&nbsp;
were igneous, having formed from the cooling of&nbsp;&nbsp;
liquid magma and pushed up to the surface. Over&nbsp;
long periods of time, rain and wind would break&nbsp;&nbsp;
these rocks apart and transport their sediments to&nbsp;
the oceans, where they would be buried and later&nbsp;&nbsp;
lithified into sedimentary rocks. In parts of&nbsp;
the world where two plates were moving together,&nbsp;&nbsp;
these original igneous rocks were metamorphosed by&nbsp;
the heat and pressure of collision, and uplifted&nbsp;&nbsp;
to form mountains containing metamorphic rock. And&nbsp;
just as before, these rocks were broken down and&nbsp;&nbsp;
their sediments transported to sedimentary&nbsp;
basins. These new sedimentary rocks would&nbsp;&nbsp;
later be uplifted and exhumed, where they would&nbsp;
again be attacked by the wind and rain, broken&nbsp;&nbsp;
into sediment, and transported to a brand-new&nbsp;
sedimentary basin. In parts of the world that&nbsp;&nbsp;
were experiencing subduction, the original rock&nbsp;
in the subducting slab was returned to the mantle,&nbsp;&nbsp;
where it would presumably sink to D’’ and later&nbsp;
rise to form a mantle plume, which would melt,&nbsp;&nbsp;
create a volcano, and erupt lava to form a&nbsp;
brand-new igneous rock that perhaps shares&nbsp;&nbsp;
a few silicon or oxygen atoms in common with&nbsp;
the rock from the previous iteration of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
cycle. And then the whole show begins again.
It is by these processes, which are collectively&nbsp;&nbsp;
referred to as the rock cycle, that Earth&nbsp;
continuously recycles geologic material,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and in some cases, mineral grains from&nbsp;
the primordial Earth have survived over&nbsp;&nbsp;
4 billion years of the rock cycle, and exist&nbsp;
today as parts of ancient sedimentary rocks.&nbsp;&nbsp;
A famous formation in Australia, called the Jack&nbsp;
Hills Conglomerate, contains detrital zircon,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is a particularly durable mineral,&nbsp;
that is over 4.4 billion years old.&nbsp;
We can look at an image like this to summarize&nbsp;
all of the possibilities within the rock cycle&nbsp;&nbsp;
at once. We start with magma, which cools&nbsp;
to form igneous rock. This could melt to&nbsp;&nbsp;
form magma again, or it could experience&nbsp;
weathering and erosion to form sediments,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which can compact and cement to form sedimentary&nbsp;
rock. Igneous rock can also experience high heat&nbsp;&nbsp;
and pressure to form metamorphic rock.&nbsp;
Sedimentary rocks can do the same thing,&nbsp;&nbsp;
if they are also subjected to such high heats&nbsp;
and pressures. Then both sedimentary rock and&nbsp;&nbsp;
metamorphic rock can experience weathering and&nbsp;
erosion to produce sediments, just like igneous&nbsp;&nbsp;
rock, and this sediment will eventually compact to&nbsp;
form still more sedimentary rock. And metamorphic&nbsp;&nbsp;
rock can also melt, just like igneous rock, to&nbsp;
produce magma, and return to the source of it all.&nbsp;
In describing the rock cycle, and in talking&nbsp;
about the production of sediments in general,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we mentioned weathering many times, but we haven’t&nbsp;&nbsp;
gone into much detail regarding these&nbsp;
processes. So, let’s move forward and&nbsp;&nbsp;
talk about precisely how weathering and&nbsp;
erosion transform rocks into sediment.
