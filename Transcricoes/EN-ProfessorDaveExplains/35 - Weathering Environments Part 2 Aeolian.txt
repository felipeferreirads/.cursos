In the previous tutorial we learned about&nbsp;
fluvial processes, which primarily have to&nbsp;&nbsp;
do with the weathering effect of flowing water on&nbsp;
rocks and minerals. Aeolian weathering involves&nbsp;&nbsp;
the transportation, erosion, and deposition of&nbsp;
sand and silt by wind. The term aeolian comes&nbsp;&nbsp;
from the Greek god of wind, Aeolus. The movement&nbsp;
of sediment by wind is much like the movement of&nbsp;&nbsp;
sediment by water, requiring a certain velocity&nbsp;
to move a certain grain size. Due to the nearly&nbsp;&nbsp;
one-thousand-fold lower density of air when&nbsp;
compared with water, the wind velocities found&nbsp;&nbsp;
on Earth are unable to carry any sediment larger&nbsp;
than coarse sand. But here, just like with fluvial&nbsp;&nbsp;
environments, sediment can be transported&nbsp;
by traction, saltation, and suspension.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Sediments larger than 500 microns are rolled&nbsp;
along the ground and slowly “creep” forward.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Sand-sized sediments move in a series of&nbsp;
short hops called saltation that knocks&nbsp;&nbsp;
off small silt-sized particles into the wind.&nbsp;
Very small sediments, less than 70 microns,&nbsp;&nbsp;
are either suspended in the air for&nbsp;
short distances or remain in suspension.&nbsp;
Wind erosion is caused by two processes: abrasion&nbsp;
and deflation. Abrasion is a mechanical weathering&nbsp;&nbsp;
process that causes larger particles to break&nbsp;
down into smaller ones from repeated impacts&nbsp;&nbsp;
by sediment suspended in the air. Ventifacts are&nbsp;
pits, etched lines, grooves, or even polish marks&nbsp;&nbsp;
on larger rocks caused by abrasion. Deflation&nbsp;
is the removal of loose sediment from an area&nbsp;&nbsp;
by wind. Once sediment has been eroded and&nbsp;
transported by wind, it is deposited in a&nbsp;&nbsp;
variety of landforms in a way that depends on its&nbsp;
grain size. Sand forms ripples and dunes, which&nbsp;&nbsp;
are basically different-sized versions of the same&nbsp;
thing. In aeolian environments, ripples often form&nbsp;&nbsp;
on the backs of dunes. Any sediment smaller than&nbsp;
sand simply forms parallel, horizontal deposits.&nbsp;
Sand dunes come in four main types: transverse,&nbsp;
parabolic, longitudinal, and star. The type of&nbsp;&nbsp;
sand dune that forms is dependent upon the&nbsp;
wind direction. Transverse and parabolic&nbsp;&nbsp;
dunes are similar to ripples, in that they form&nbsp;
perpendicular to the prevailing wind direction,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which mostly blows in one primary direction. The&nbsp;
difference between the two is that transverse&nbsp;&nbsp;
dunes have straight crests and parabolic dunes&nbsp;
have parabolic, or curved crests. In these dunes,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sand is eroded on the windward side, because&nbsp;
the velocity is higher. The sand is transported&nbsp;&nbsp;
over the crest, and deposited on the steeper&nbsp;
leeward, or lee side, where the wind blows more&nbsp;&nbsp;
slowly. On the other hand, longitudinal dunes&nbsp;
form parallel to the direction of the wind,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is also the direction that the sand&nbsp;
is transported. Longitudinal dunes tend to&nbsp;&nbsp;
form in areas where there are two dominant wind&nbsp;
directions that are separated by about 90 degrees.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And finally, star dunes have a starfish shape and&nbsp;
occur in environments that have more than two main&nbsp;&nbsp;
wind directions. For those who were not even aware&nbsp;
that multiple types of sand dunes were possible,&nbsp;&nbsp;
this could give anyone a lot more to think&nbsp;
about when looking at a landscape like this,&nbsp;&nbsp;
imagining all of the weathering that is going&nbsp;
on at all times to produce these structures.&nbsp;
Interestingly, beyond being a widespread feature&nbsp;
of deserts on Earth, dunes also appear to occur on&nbsp;&nbsp;
other worlds in the solar system. Though Mars&nbsp;
has a very thin atmosphere, it has sufficient&nbsp;&nbsp;
wind to physically weather its basaltic bedrock&nbsp;
and mold the resulting sediment into dunes. This&nbsp;&nbsp;
image shows Martian barchan dunes, a type of&nbsp;
parabolic dune. As we can immediately surmise,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the wind is blowing toward the upper right corner.&nbsp;
Additionally, notice the smaller longitudinal&nbsp;&nbsp;
dunes on the backs of some of the larger dunes.
An even more exotic example of extraterrestrial&nbsp;&nbsp;
dunes was found during the 1997 Cassini–Huygens&nbsp;
mission, which provided fascinating observations&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Saturn’s moon Titan. The Cassini probe&nbsp;
collected hundreds of high-resolution images&nbsp;&nbsp;
of Titan’s surface, revealing the presence of&nbsp;
lakes that appear to be fed by an extensive system&nbsp;&nbsp;
of river channels, much like Earth. However,&nbsp;
unlike Earth, Titan’s lakes are filled with&nbsp;&nbsp;
liquid hydrocarbons, likely a mix of methane and&nbsp;
ethane. The presence of lakes and river channels&nbsp;&nbsp;
indicates that Titan has a hydrological cycle, but&nbsp;
with methane and ethane instead of water. Cassini&nbsp;&nbsp;
also found large subaerial dune fields in Titan’s&nbsp;
lower latitudes. These dunes are not composed of&nbsp;&nbsp;
silicate sand like on Earth, but small fragments&nbsp;
of water ice derived from Titan’s bedrock.&nbsp;
