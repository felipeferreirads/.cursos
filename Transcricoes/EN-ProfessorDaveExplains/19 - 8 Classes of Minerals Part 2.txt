In the previous tutorial we covered the first four&nbsp;
classes of minerals, those being native elements,&nbsp;&nbsp;
oxides, halides, and sulfides. The other&nbsp;
four mineral classes that we will discuss&nbsp;&nbsp;
can all be described as oxyanions, or minerals&nbsp;
with polyatomic anions composed of some atom&nbsp;&nbsp;
covalently bonded to oxygen atoms. The most&nbsp;
important class of these minerals by far is&nbsp;&nbsp;
the silicates, but these will require several&nbsp;
tutorials unto themselves. There are three other&nbsp;&nbsp;
classes of minerals containing oxyanions, those&nbsp;
being the carbonates, sulfates, and phosphates,&nbsp;&nbsp;
so these are the ones we will focus on here.
Carbonates are minerals that contain the carbonate&nbsp;&nbsp;
anion, CO32-, and this is a representative&nbsp;
group for other similar anion complexes&nbsp;&nbsp;
such as nitrates and borates, the latter being a&nbsp;
bit more complex. Carbonates are commonly found in&nbsp;&nbsp;
biogenic sedimentary rocks and evaporite deposits,&nbsp;
or rocks composed of minerals formed through the&nbsp;&nbsp;
evaporation of natural waters. The most common&nbsp;
carbonate mineral by far is calcite, or calcium&nbsp;&nbsp;
carbonate. Moreover, calcite happens to be the&nbsp;
most abundant non-silicate mineral in the crust.&nbsp;&nbsp;
There are actually two forms of calcium carbonate,&nbsp;
calcite, which is in a rhombohedral form,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and aragonite, which is in an orthorhombic, or&nbsp;
rectangular form. Limestone is composed primarily&nbsp;&nbsp;
of calcite, which is soluble in acidic solutions&nbsp;
like rainwater; hence, areas with limestone&nbsp;&nbsp;
bedrock commonly form large cave systems, such as&nbsp;
Mammoth Cave in central Kentucky. Limestone caves&nbsp;&nbsp;
typically have large deposits of calcite crystals&nbsp;
located throughout known as speleothems. They&nbsp;&nbsp;
include stalactites, stalagmites, and flowstone.&nbsp;
Speleothems are formed by groundwater that&nbsp;&nbsp;
degasses, or releases dissolved CO2, upon entering&nbsp;
the cave’s atmosphere, which is typically aided&nbsp;&nbsp;
by agitation through such processes as flowing&nbsp;
over a rough surface or dripping onto the floor.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The carbonate minerals are a fundamental component&nbsp;
of the carbon cycle, representing an enormous sink&nbsp;&nbsp;
for atmospheric carbon dioxide, especially when&nbsp;
they form in the ocean and sink to the bottom.&nbsp;&nbsp;
More on that in the upcoming ecology series.
Sulfates contain the sulfate anion, SO42-, and&nbsp;&nbsp;
this is a representative group for other similar&nbsp;
anion complexes such as selenates, chromates, and&nbsp;&nbsp;
molybdates. Gypsum, or hydrated calcium sulfate,&nbsp;
is a common sulfate mineral that has been used for&nbsp;&nbsp;
building and construction since ancient times. The&nbsp;
fine-grained variety of gypsum, called alabaster,&nbsp;&nbsp;
appears in canopic jars from ancient Egypt.&nbsp;
Gypsum can be dehydrated into anhydrite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or calcium sulfate when heated, and the&nbsp;
two minerals are often closely associated.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Like calcite, gypsum and anhydrite can&nbsp;
be precipitated from evaporating seawater&nbsp;&nbsp;
and dissolved out of bedrock to form large caves.
Phosphates contain the phosphate anion, PO43-.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This class is also representative of other&nbsp;
similarly structured anionic complexes such&nbsp;&nbsp;
as arsenates and vanadates. A commonly encountered&nbsp;
group of phosphate minerals is the apatite group,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which has the chemical formula Ca5(PO4)3(X)2,&nbsp;
where a combination or pure fraction of chlorine,&nbsp;&nbsp;
fluorine, and/or hydroxyls can fill the&nbsp;
X-site. When a combination of one or more&nbsp;&nbsp;
elements can fill a specific crystallographic&nbsp;
site in a mineral, it is called solid solution;&nbsp;&nbsp;
hence, it is said that there is solid solution&nbsp;
between each endmember of the apatite group,&nbsp;&nbsp;
those being hydroxyapatite, chlorapatite,&nbsp;
and fluorapatite. Much of your daily calcium&nbsp;&nbsp;
intake from foods and vitamins goes toward&nbsp;
the construction of hydroxyapatite for your&nbsp;&nbsp;
bones and teeth. In geology, apatite can&nbsp;
be an accessory mineral in igneous rocks&nbsp;&nbsp;
or a component of certain sedimentary rocks that&nbsp;
are rich in skeletal fragments like fish bones.&nbsp;
Our final class to discuss is the mineraloids,&nbsp;
which are not really minerals since they are not&nbsp;&nbsp;
crystalline. Since mineraloids are naturally&nbsp;
occurring substances found in rocks, they&nbsp;&nbsp;
are often mistaken for minerals. Some examples&nbsp;
include opal, which is amorphous hydrated silica,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and obsidian, or volcanic glass. Obsidian&nbsp;
forms when a felsic lava is rapidly cooled,&nbsp;&nbsp;
leaving insufficient time for the atoms to&nbsp;
migrate to form a regular, repeating structure.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Remember when we talked about siliceous&nbsp;
ooze in the tutorial on oceanic crust?&nbsp;&nbsp;
Well, opal is what siliceous ooze transforms&nbsp;
into with time, and over even more time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
opal converts to microcrystalline quartz,&nbsp;
forming the sedimentary rock, chert.&nbsp;
So that covers 7 out of the 8 classes&nbsp;
of minerals, plus the mineraloids.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The one class that we haven’t covered yet,&nbsp;
silicates, will have to be covered separately&nbsp;&nbsp;
due to its abundance and importance, so let’s&nbsp;
move forward and check out silicates next.
