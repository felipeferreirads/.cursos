Over the previous two tutorials, we covered&nbsp;
7 out of the 8 classes of minerals. That&nbsp;&nbsp;
leaves just one more, and it’s the most important&nbsp;
class, including the majority of the minerals in&nbsp;&nbsp;
Earth’s crust and upper mantle. We are talking&nbsp;
about silicates. All silicates are made of the&nbsp;&nbsp;
same basic building block, the silica tetrahedron,&nbsp;
which is simply a silicate, or SiO44- anion. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
structure of the silica tetrahedra is a central&nbsp;
silicon atom that is bonded to four oxygen atoms.&nbsp;&nbsp;
You can think of it as a pyramid, with a silicon&nbsp;
atom in the center and an oxygen at each corner.&nbsp;
Silicate minerals are divided into subclasses&nbsp;
based on their arrangement and linkage of silica&nbsp;&nbsp;
tetrahedra, which can range from a single,&nbsp;
isolated tetrahedron bonded to a metal,&nbsp;&nbsp;
to a complex three-dimensional framework&nbsp;
of silica tetrahedra. The ability of silica&nbsp;&nbsp;
tetrahedra to link together, or polymerize, to&nbsp;
form a diverse array of intricate structures&nbsp;&nbsp;
stems from its unique mesodemic bonding, where&nbsp;
each of the oxygen anions has one of its two&nbsp;&nbsp;
valence charges satisfied by bonding with its&nbsp;
tetrahedron’s central silicon atom, thus leaving&nbsp;&nbsp;
a remaining charge of negative one at each corner&nbsp;
of the tetrahedron that is available for bonding.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Because of this, a given silica tetrahedron can&nbsp;
bond with up to four other silica tetrahedra,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or none at all. The structural complexity of&nbsp;
the silicate minerals increases with the degree&nbsp;&nbsp;
of polymerization, and even though quartz, or&nbsp;
SiO2, is about as simple as it gets chemically,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it is structurally very complex, with its&nbsp;
three-dimensional framework of silica tetrahedra.&nbsp;&nbsp;
We will first discuss the silicates that do&nbsp;
not polymerize at all, the orthosilicates.&nbsp;
Orthosilicates, also called nesosilicates,&nbsp;
are the simplest subclass of silicates,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as the silica tetrahedra are not polymerized,&nbsp;
meaning there is no sharing of oxygen atoms&nbsp;&nbsp;
between silica tetrahedra. Instead, the silica&nbsp;
tetrahedra are linked by bonding with cations,&nbsp;&nbsp;
like magnesium, iron, calcium, and aluminum.&nbsp;
Unlike silicon, which bonds to four oxygen&nbsp;&nbsp;
anions to form tetrahedra, larger cations&nbsp;
like magnesium and iron bond, or coordinate&nbsp;&nbsp;
with 6 oxygen anions to form octahedra. Moreover,&nbsp;
even larger cations, like the alkali metals and&nbsp;&nbsp;
calcium, coordinate with eight or more oxygen&nbsp;
atoms, depending on the mineral’s structure.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Knowing that the coordination of an ion&nbsp;
is dependent on its radius, it is easy to&nbsp;&nbsp;
predict which atoms can substitute for others in&nbsp;
a crystal’s lattice. This brings us to the most&nbsp;&nbsp;
common type of orthosilicate minerals, olivine.&nbsp;
Olivine has two octahedral sites that contain&nbsp;&nbsp;
medium-sized divalent cations, in addition to its&nbsp;
tetrahedral silicon. In a typical olivine, the&nbsp;&nbsp;
octahedral sites contain a mixture of magnesium&nbsp;
and ferrous iron, with more magnesium than iron.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Geologists call this mixing of different ions on&nbsp;
a crystallographic site, solid solution. In the&nbsp;&nbsp;
previous tutorial, we discussed endmembers, which&nbsp;
are minerals that represent the chemical extremes&nbsp;&nbsp;
of a group. In the olivine group, fayalite, or&nbsp;
Fe2SiO4, is the iron endmember, and forsterite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
or Mg2SiO4, is the magnesium endmember, with&nbsp;
complete solid solution existing between them.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Another important group of orthosilicates&nbsp;
is garnet, which comes in a wide variety of&nbsp;&nbsp;
compositions with extensive solid solution. Its&nbsp;
base formula is X3Y2(SiO4)3, where the X-site&nbsp;&nbsp;
is an eight-fold site that can be occupied by&nbsp;
calcium, magnesium, ferrous iron, and manganese,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and the Y-site is a six-fold site that&nbsp;
typically contains aluminum and ferric iron.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The last group of orthosilicates we&nbsp;
will discuss are the aluminosilicates,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which have a chemical formula&nbsp;
of Al2SiO5. Unlike the others,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the minerals in this group all have the exact&nbsp;
same formula. Where they differ is their crystal&nbsp;&nbsp;
structure. Minerals with the same formula but&nbsp;
different structures are called polymorphs,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with the temperature and pressure of formation&nbsp;
influencing which polymorph crystallizes.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For the aluminosilicates, kyanite is the&nbsp;
high-pressure phase, sillimanite is the&nbsp;&nbsp;
high temperature phase, and andalusite is&nbsp;
the low temperature-low pressure phase.&nbsp;
Next are the disilicates, also called&nbsp;
sorosilicates, which are composed of&nbsp;&nbsp;
pairs of silica tetrahedra that share one oxygen&nbsp;
atom and have the basic structural unit Si2O76-.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Due to its similarity in size and charge with the&nbsp;
silicon cation, aluminum sometimes substitutes in,&nbsp;&nbsp;
forming aluminum tetrahedra. When aluminum, which&nbsp;
has a charge of 3+, substitutes for silicon,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which has a charge of 4+, the mineral must somehow&nbsp;
balance out this gained negative charge. This can&nbsp;&nbsp;
be accomplished by picking up a hydrogen ion or&nbsp;
substituting a lower charged cation, like sodium,&nbsp;&nbsp;
for a higher charged one, like calcium. The most&nbsp;
common sorosilicates are in the epidote group,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which contain the minerals zoisite, epidote, and&nbsp;
allanite. These minerals are chemically complex,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with three octahedral sites, typically&nbsp;
containing aluminum and ferric iron,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and a distorted 7-to-11-fold site that contains&nbsp;
larger cations like calcium. The mineral allanite&nbsp;&nbsp;
is a known for taking up rare earth elements like&nbsp;
yttrium and cerium into its structure, where they&nbsp;&nbsp;
substitute for calcium in the large-cation&nbsp;
site, making it a valuable ore material.&nbsp;
Ring silicates, also called cyclosilicates, are&nbsp;
composed of silica tetrahedra that share two&nbsp;&nbsp;
oxygen atoms and have a silicon to oxygen&nbsp;
ratio of 1 to 3. Their name is derived from&nbsp;&nbsp;
their structure, which forms rings of six&nbsp;
silica tetrahedra stacked atop one another.&nbsp;&nbsp;
They are a small group, with only a handful&nbsp;
of commonly-occurring minerals, including&nbsp;&nbsp;
the popular gemstones beryl and tourmaline, with&nbsp;
beryl also being an important ore of beryllium.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Let’s consider the structure of beryl,&nbsp;
which has the chemical formula Al2Be3Si6O18.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Between its stacked 6-membered rings of&nbsp;
silica tetrahedra are sheets of laterally&nbsp;&nbsp;
linked 12-membered rings composed of alternating&nbsp;
aluminum octahedra and beryllium tetrahedra that&nbsp;&nbsp;
cross-link the rings of silica tetrahedra. This&nbsp;
creates an intricate three-dimensional framework,&nbsp;&nbsp;
like that of a framework silicate, a group we will&nbsp;
discuss in the next tutorial. The stacked rings&nbsp;&nbsp;
of tetrahedra create long cylindrical channels&nbsp;
that are commonly filled with large cations,&nbsp;&nbsp;
like sodium, potassium, and cesium, which creates&nbsp;
a net positive charge that must be balanced.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This is achieved by substituting divalent cations,&nbsp;&nbsp;
like chromium and ferrous iron for aluminum in&nbsp;
the octahedral site. The gemstones emerald and&nbsp;&nbsp;
aquamarine are varieties of beryl, containing&nbsp;
trace amounts of chromium and iron respectively,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are responsible for their color.
That covers three of the subclasses of&nbsp;&nbsp;
silicates, but there are several more, so&nbsp;
let’s move forward and check out the rest.
