Now that we know some things about identifying&nbsp;
minerals based on their physical characteristics,&nbsp;&nbsp;
we should learn how to group similar minerals&nbsp;
together based on their chemistry. This is&nbsp;&nbsp;
important because one of the major differences&nbsp;
between the different types of mineral-forming&nbsp;&nbsp;
geological environments is their chemistry.
For example, mafic rocks, which are depleted&nbsp;&nbsp;
in silica and rich in calcium, magnesium,&nbsp;
and iron, tend to be composed of different&nbsp;&nbsp;
classes of minerals than felsic rocks, which&nbsp;
are rich in silica, aluminum, and sodium,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and depleted in magnesium and iron.&nbsp;
In addition, a mineral’s chemistry&nbsp;&nbsp;
directly affects its lattice structure due to&nbsp;
differences in atomic radii and bond lengths.&nbsp;&nbsp;
This means that from a clear macroscopic property,&nbsp;
like a mineral’s cleavage, we can potentially know&nbsp;&nbsp;
something about its structure, which we can relate&nbsp;
to its chemistry, and which we can use to know&nbsp;&nbsp;
something about the geologic environment in which&nbsp;
it formed. Of course, this does not always work,&nbsp;&nbsp;
however it is a simple logical framework that&nbsp;
geologists use to determine a rock’s history.&nbsp;
Minerals are divided into eight classes&nbsp;
based primarily on the dominant anion&nbsp;&nbsp;
in their chemical formula. The eight classes&nbsp;
are as follows: native elements, oxides,&nbsp;&nbsp;
halides, sulfides, carbonates, sulfates,&nbsp;
phosphates, and silicates. Moreover,&nbsp;&nbsp;
an additional class, mineraloids, is reserved for&nbsp;
geologic materials that lack a crystal structure,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and therefore are not minerals. Many of these&nbsp;
terms should already be familiar from our study&nbsp;&nbsp;
of chemistry, but either way, let’s make sure&nbsp;
we contextualize them in our study of geology.&nbsp;
Let’s begin with the simplest class of&nbsp;
minerals: native elements. As the name suggests,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they are naturally occurring elements that&nbsp;
have a crystalline structure. They are divided&nbsp;&nbsp;
into metals, semimetals, and nonmetals. Native&nbsp;
metals will be of the greatest interest to us,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as they form most of the native elements. Remember&nbsp;
that what makes a metal a metal is the propensity&nbsp;&nbsp;
to have delocalized valence electrons that are&nbsp;
shared among all its atoms. Most native elements&nbsp;&nbsp;
are relatively unreactive, which is evidenced by&nbsp;
their existence in such oxidizing environments&nbsp;&nbsp;
as Earth’s surface, where oxygen and water are&nbsp;
readily available to form oxides and hydroxides.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Some examples of native metals are copper, gold,&nbsp;
silver, and platinum. The native semimetals&nbsp;&nbsp;
are bismuth, antimony, arsenic, selenium, and&nbsp;
tellurium. And the only two native nonmetals&nbsp;&nbsp;
are sulfur and carbon. There is an entire field&nbsp;
of science called metallurgy that is completely&nbsp;&nbsp;
based on the study of metals. One can imagine&nbsp;
the importance of metallurgy in the modern&nbsp;&nbsp;
age when considering all of the different&nbsp;
properties of metals that are required for&nbsp;&nbsp;
specific applications, such as electrical&nbsp;
conductivity, superconductivity, rigidity,&nbsp;&nbsp;
flexibility, magnetism, and high melting point.&nbsp;
For example, electric heating elements require&nbsp;&nbsp;
an alloy that is ductile with a high electrical&nbsp;
resistance and melting point, whereas solder,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which is used to connect wires to circuit boards,&nbsp;
must have a low melting point and low resistivity.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Furthermore, you wouldn’t even be able to watch&nbsp;
this video without the single-crystal silicon in&nbsp;&nbsp;
your computer’s central processing unit. Thus, the&nbsp;
incredible importance of understanding minerals.&nbsp;
Since most minerals contain oxygen,&nbsp;
such as with silicates and carbonates,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the oxide class is restricted to minerals where&nbsp;
one or more type of cation is ionically bonded&nbsp;&nbsp;
only to oxygen. Oxide minerals can be broken down&nbsp;
into two groups: simple oxides, which only contain&nbsp;&nbsp;
cations of identical charge, and the spinels,&nbsp;
which contain cations of dissimilar charge.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The simple oxides can be further grouped based&nbsp;
on the charge of their cation, which is usually&nbsp;&nbsp;
between one and four. Some examples of simple&nbsp;
oxide minerals are cuprite, a monovalent copper&nbsp;&nbsp;
oxide, periclase, a divalent magnesium oxide,&nbsp;
hematite, a trivalent iron oxide, and rutile,&nbsp;&nbsp;
a tetravalent titanium oxide. Another instantly&nbsp;
recognizable monovalent oxide mineral is hydrogen&nbsp;&nbsp;
oxide, which is more commonly known as ice. The&nbsp;
spinels are a bit more complicated, having both&nbsp;&nbsp;
a divalent and trivalent cation in their chemical&nbsp;
formula. Magnetite is an iron oxide like hematite,&nbsp;&nbsp;
but it contains both ferric and ferrous iron,&nbsp;
having a chemical formula of (Fe3+)2(Fe2+)(O2-)4.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The difference between magnetite and hematite is&nbsp;
clearly highlighted by their magnetic properties,&nbsp;&nbsp;
as magnetite is strongly attracted to a&nbsp;
magnetic field, whereas hematite is not.&nbsp;
An important subgroup of the&nbsp;
oxides is the hydroxides,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are composed of cations that&nbsp;
are bonded to a hydroxide ion.&nbsp;&nbsp;
They are commonly produced by chemical weathering,&nbsp;
or alteration of other minerals as they react with&nbsp;&nbsp;
precipitation and groundwater near the surface.&nbsp;
Bauxite, which is the dominant ore of aluminum,&nbsp;&nbsp;
is composed of several aluminum oxy-hydroxide&nbsp;
minerals that form in tropical environments where&nbsp;&nbsp;
abundant precipitation and high temperatures&nbsp;
intensely leach everything out of the rocks&nbsp;&nbsp;
except for aluminum, which is one of the most&nbsp;
immobile and insoluble geologic materials.&nbsp;
Another simple class of minerals&nbsp;
with ionic bonding is the halides,&nbsp;&nbsp;
which are composed of a cation bonded to a&nbsp;
halogen, typically fluorine or chlorine. The&nbsp;&nbsp;
halides are the most ionic of the minerals and are&nbsp;
usually very soluble in water. Halite, or sodium&nbsp;&nbsp;
chloride, is a common halide mineral and it often&nbsp;
precipitates during the evaporation of seawater.&nbsp;
Our final class of minerals that contain a&nbsp;
single anion are the sulfides. Unlike the&nbsp;&nbsp;
oxides and halides, the bonding in sulfides is&nbsp;
very complex and can range from ionic-covalent to&nbsp;&nbsp;
metallic-covalent depending on what it is bonding&nbsp;
with. Most sulfide minerals are composed of sulfur&nbsp;&nbsp;
bonded to a transition metal, and often display&nbsp;
many of the properties of metals. Incidentally,&nbsp;&nbsp;
both galena, lead sulfide, and pyrite, iron&nbsp;
disulfide, are semiconductors with a metallic&nbsp;&nbsp;
luster. Many sulfide minerals are both associated&nbsp;
with and hosts of numerous metallic ore deposits.&nbsp;&nbsp;
For example, the Bushveld Intrusion, which is a&nbsp;
large intrusive igneous formation in South Africa,&nbsp;&nbsp;
possesses a layer of sulfide minerals that&nbsp;
contain 65 percent of the world’s reserve&nbsp;&nbsp;
of platinum group elements.
So that covers four out of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the eight classes of minerals. Let’s move&nbsp;
forward and check out the other half now.
